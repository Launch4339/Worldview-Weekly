TITLE: Worldview Weekly — October 22, 2026

Welcome to Worldview Weekly, an analytical complement to The Economist. This week, we look beneath the headline measure. A total can be accurate and still conceal the question that matters: who participates, who carries risk, which capabilities endure, and what gets lost. We begin with economics and political institutions, then move to science, culture, and development. For each reading, we distinguish the author’s argument from our own analysis. The examples introduced as hypothetical are illustrations, not reported events. Publisher links and the reading list are in the written feed.

BUSINESS, ECONOMICS, AND GLOBAL POLITICS

Eurosclerosis or US Decline?

Our first economics reading is Dominik Leusder’s October ninth essay for Phenomenal World. His central objection is methodological: claims that Europe is falling dramatically behind America depend on which output, price and working-time measures are chosen. Exchange-rate comparisons can mislead, while different purchasing-power series imply different historical trajectories. He also asks how distribution and expensive services affect apparent prosperity. This is a forceful intervention, not a settled statistical verdict. Its best contribution is to make the reader interrogate the measuring instrument before accepting the geopolitical conclusion.

Here is a separate thought experiment. Imagine two otherwise identical societies. In one, residents work forty hours a week and buy most services individually. In the other, residents work thirty-two hours and receive some services collectively. Suppose the first records greater cash income. That observation cannot, on its own, tell us which resident has more freedom, greater security or a better life. But neither does it establish that the second society could mobilize the same resources in an emergency. Welfare and capacity are different questions. We should resist trying to answer both with one scoreboard.

This distinction matters when a ranking becomes a prescription. Before changing taxes, labor protections or public institutions to imitate a supposedly superior economy, ask what outcome is being optimized. Is the objective household consumption, leisure, technological leadership, fiscal resilience or military capacity? Those goals can overlap without being interchangeable. An improvement on one measure can involve a sacrifice on another. Making that tradeoff explicit is more honest than presenting a political choice as something compelled by arithmetic.

A useful discipline is to ask for three separate demonstrations. First, establish that the gap is real using a measure appropriate to the question. Second, establish which mechanism produced it. Third, establish that the proposed reform actually changes that mechanism at an acceptable cost. The second and third steps do not follow automatically from the first. Even a perfectly measured difference would not identify its cause.

Our takeaway is therefore not that Europe wins or America loses. It is that confident rankings deserve slower reasoning. The most useful counterweight to a grand decline story may be an insistence on specifying the question before choosing the statistic.

Indonesia is dodging the dollar in trade, not in debt

Ramkishen Rajan, a professor at the National University of Singapore, writes in the Lowy Institute’s Interpreter on October twenty-first. His argument distinguishes payment arrangements from balance-sheet exposure. Indonesia’s local-currency settlement is expanding, but much private external borrowing remains dollar-denominated. Gross debt and reserve totals do not reveal borrowers’ net funding needs or hedges. Rajan calls for better visibility into those exposures. The article offers a financial plumbing explanation rather than treating de-dollarization as a single geopolitical switch.

Consider an invented company that sells household appliances. It changes the currency on next month’s invoices, but still owes a lender money under a five-year contract written in another currency. The invoice change may make trading cheaper or simpler. It does not rewrite the loan. Now suppose the company’s income becomes unpredictable precisely when the lender expects repayment. We would want to know the repayment schedule, the available cash, the quality of any protection and who stands behind that protection. A label describing the company’s preferred trading currency would not answer those questions.

This thought experiment suggests a broader way of reading economic sovereignty claims. Instead of asking whether a country has escaped dependence, ask where dependence has moved. Does it sit in everyday payments, emergency liquidity, refinancing, insurance, technology or legal enforcement? Progress in one layer can coexist with vulnerability in another. That is not an argument against diversification. It is an argument for evaluating each layer separately.

We should also distinguish a useful precaution from a prediction. Identifying an exposure is not the same as predicting a crisis. A household can have a variable-rate mortgage without defaulting; a firm can have foreign obligations and manage them well. The analytical question is whether an adverse combination of events would overwhelm the buffers, not whether the exposure sounds alarming in isolation.

For the reader, the practical habit is to replace a binary question with a sequence. What must be paid? When? From which revenue stream? Under which contingency does the plan fail? Who can supply the missing resource then? These are deliberately general questions, not investment advice or a forecast for Indonesia. They convert a dramatic narrative about currency rivalry into a testable account of resilience. The distinction between changing the interface and changing the underlying obligation is useful far beyond international finance.

Investment screening reshapes rather than shrinks foreign investment

Feicheng Wang and Vera Eichenauer’s October twenty-first VoxEU column examines investment-screening regimes across forty-three countries. They report fewer cross-border acquisitions after screening begins, particularly minority investments, but no statistically detectable fall in aggregate deal value. Larger controlling deals become relatively more important. Their interpretation emphasizes uncertainty and compliance costs alongside security restrictions. The historical evidence is not a forecast for every new screening rule, and a statistically insignificant aggregate effect is not proof that economic costs are absent.

An original analogy helps clarify what this kind of result can mean. Imagine a town that introduces a fixed inspection charge for every new shop. A small independent seller may abandon an opening, while a large chain can absorb the same charge. Total retail investment could remain constant if the chain builds one larger store. Yet the town would have a different ownership structure, a different range of businesses and perhaps a different pattern of bargaining power. The unchanged total would be informative, but incomplete.

Now add a legitimate safety purpose. Some inspections may prevent serious harm. It would be wrong to conclude that all inspections are wasteful because small operators find them expensive. Equally, invoking safety does not remove the need to examine design. We would want to know whether the same protection can be achieved with clearer criteria, less uncertainty or a process proportionate to the risk. Those are empirical and administrative questions, not a choice between having rules and having none.

The broader lesson is about the unit of evaluation. A policy may preserve the volume of an activity while changing who is able to participate. That distributional change could matter even when the headline indicator looks stable. Conversely, a fall in the number of transactions need not imply an equally large fall in productive capacity. Counting, valuing and examining control provide different windows onto the same system.

When assessing an economic-security measure, we therefore need two ledgers. One records the intended reduction in risk. The other records changes in opportunity, competition and administrative burden. Neither ledger should be assumed empty. A rigorous defense of screening would demonstrate benefits in the first while taking costs in the second seriously. A rigorous criticism would do the reverse without denying genuine security problems. This article earns its place because it makes the outcomes less binary, and therefore makes the policy debate more demanding.

Brazilian Crossroads

The October tenth Phenomenal World interview with political scientist Marcos Nobre offers an institutional account of Brazil’s political conflict. Nobre argues that a more autonomous congressional bargaining bloc now confronts a traditional Workers’ Party and a digitally organized far right. He treats online organization as more than messaging: it can sustain participation and authority between elections. His favorable assessment of Lula and strongly critical account of opponents are explicit perspectives. Read this as an interpretive model, not neutral consensus or an election prediction.

A question worth taking beyond Brazil is what it means to possess a majority. Imagine a leader elected by most voters who must negotiate separately with legislators controlling the budget. The electoral mandate is real, but it does not automatically determine what gets funded. Now imagine that the legislators face different incentives from the executive: local patronage, committee influence or the next coalition bargain. A disappointing policy outcome could reflect that structure rather than simply a leader’s competence or sincerity. That hypothetical does not excuse poor leadership; it identifies additional mechanisms to investigate.

A second question concerns organization. Suppose one movement meets people only during campaigns while another gives them recurring opportunities to participate in social life. Even if both distribute the same number of political messages, their relationships with supporters might be very different. We should ask whether participation produces durable obligations, a shared identity, a route to leadership or merely occasional attention. Counting followers would be a poor substitute for answering those questions.

There is also a caution here. An explanation can be illuminating without being complete. If a framework describes one side primarily as democratic and the other primarily as a threat, it may clarify important stakes while leaving other motivations underexamined. A careful reader should ask what evidence would challenge the model. Could institutional incentives explain conduct that the interview attributes to ideology? Could economic disappointment explain choices attributed to organizational strength? More than one mechanism may operate at once.

The value of this reading is that it moves us away from a contest between personalities toward a contest among organizational forms and centers of authority. The question to retain is not simply who wins the presidency. It is what a winning coalition can actually do, which other actors can block it, and how competing movements reproduce their influence between elections. Those questions remain useful after any particular result is known.

Xi’s Delhi visit: China’s new tone, old assumptions about India

Saqlain Rizve’s October twenty-second Interpreter essay asks whether warmer diplomacy changes the status perceptions underlying China–India relations. Drawing on scholarship about historical images and recognition, he contrasts enduring dismissiveness with the possibility of conditional accommodation. The article acknowledges that historical remarks cannot establish what present leaders or publics think. This is a complement to material-power explanations, not a replacement for them: the proposed mechanism concerns how actors interpret differences and what recognition they believe they deserve.

To explore the mechanism without claiming more evidence than we have, imagine two organizations negotiating a joint venture. One considers the other a useful junior partner; the other believes the relationship must be between equals. They might agree on prices, schedules and technical standards yet repeatedly clash over titles, decision rights or who announces an agreement. Those apparently symbolic disputes can matter because they reveal incompatible expectations about the relationship’s future.

But this analogy also shows why a status explanation must be tested rather than assumed. Perhaps the argument over titles is only a bargaining tactic for obtaining money. Perhaps a supposed insult is a translation error. Perhaps neither side has a stable view because different factions want different outcomes. An analyst needs evidence distinguishing these possibilities. Otherwise, psychological vocabulary can become an elegant way of describing anything after it happens.

One useful test would ask whether behavior changes when material incentives stay similar but public recognition changes. Another would compare private negotiations with public rhetoric. A third would examine whether a perception survives leadership turnover or appears only around a specific dispute. These are proposed research questions, not findings reported by the article. They illustrate how a historical interpretation could become more falsifiable.

For the weekly issue, this piece broadens the way we think about diplomatic improvement. A meeting can lower immediate friction without resolving the parties’ different pictures of their relationship. Equally, an unresolved identity dispute does not make practical cooperation impossible. We should allow for partial bargains rather than insist that every gesture proves either reconciliation or deception.

The durable insight is to separate the content of a deal from the relationship each participant thinks the deal represents. Trade, security and status can interact without moving together. Following all three requires more patience than declaring a new era after a summit, but it is more likely to reveal what has actually changed.

Zimbabwe’s Emerging Relevance in US–Africa Relations

Zimbabwean researcher Simba Ruwodo’s October fifteenth FPRI article interprets Harare’s economic diplomacy as diversification rather than a choice between Washington and Beijing. He argues that mineral opportunities become valuable through investment, infrastructure, processing and market access, and that durable engagement should serve broader development. The piece is a policy argument supported by examples, not an evaluation proving that new partnerships deliver shared gains. Its value is to foreground African bargaining agency without treating resource ownership as sufficient for prosperity.

Imagine a supplier with a valuable product and several potential customers. More customers can improve its negotiating position, but only if their offers are genuinely independent and the supplier can afford to reject a bad one. If transport, financing or processing still depends on a single bottleneck, an apparently diverse customer list may conceal a narrow set of practical choices. This is a hypothetical bargaining model, not a claim about a specific Zimbabwean project.

It suggests several questions for any proposed partnership. Which party can walk away, and at what cost? Who supplies the capital before revenues arrive? Who owns the productive knowledge afterward? Can local firms enter the next stage of the supply chain, or do they remain replaceable subcontractors? Those questions distinguish an attractive announcement from a durable expansion of capability.

There is a second distinction between a state’s bargaining power and benefits for its citizens. A government may secure a better commercial arrangement without distributing its proceeds broadly. Conversely, a modest agreement could produce useful local capabilities even if it attracts little geopolitical attention. Evaluating development requires following the gains through institutions and households, not stopping at the signature ceremony. That is an analytical caution, not an allegation about any particular deal.

The reader should also resist a comforting inversion. Rejecting the idea that smaller countries are merely pawns does not mean they face no constraints. Agency exists within limits. The interesting task is to identify where choices are meaningful, where alternatives are credible and which reforms might expand them. That requires attention to implementation rather than diplomatic language alone.

This final politics reading links back to the issue’s economics. A larger menu of partners is an opportunity, not an outcome. The test is whether it changes capabilities, resilience and the distribution of benefits. Taking a country seriously as an actor means scrutinizing its strategy and results, rather than assuming either helplessness or effortless autonomy.


SCIENCE AND TECHNOLOGY: WHAT AN AI SCORE REALLY MEASURES

John Pavlus’s October 16 Quanta interview with computer scientist Moritz Hardt asks whether AI benchmarks measure intelligence or organize competition. Hardt distinguishes unreliable absolute scores from model rankings that historically proved more robust. With language models trained on undisclosed, different data, even rankings can become misleading. His proposed response is to give competing systems comparable preparation before testing. It is a provocative research agenda, not an established cure.

For additional context, Hardt’s freely available book chapter on language-model evaluation explains two different measurement traps. Multiple-choice tests can contain clues in the wrong answers, allowing systems to exploit patterns rather than solve the intended problem. Human preference tests have a different vulnerability: a judge who cannot verify an answer may reward confidence, agreeable phrasing, or attractive presentation. Pairwise contests therefore do not automatically identify the most accurate system. Expert review can help, but is expensive and still leaves the question of which tasks deserve evaluation. Those are distinct failure modes: answering the test without possessing the intended skill, and impressing the evaluator without being correct.

Our analytical takeaway is that a benchmark is also an institution. It determines what participants have an incentive to optimize. Consider a hypothetical translation service. A test might score fluent prose highly while overlooking whether the translation preserved a crucial exception in a contract. A different test might reward literal accuracy but punish perfectly sensible local phrasing. Neither number is meaningless; neither is a universal quality score. Choosing the test requires deciding which errors matter for the actual use.

For readers following the economics of AI, this changes how to interpret a product launch. A leaderboard victory is evidence about a specified contest, not proof that a system can replace every professional represented in the test questions. The useful next questions are practical: Was the test available during development? Were competitors given comparable resources? Who judged success? Can the result be reproduced on a genuinely new task? What is the cost of a serious failure?

This complements familiar coverage of investment and productivity by examining the instrument behind many performance claims. The central uncertainty is not whether machines are improving. It is which improvements our measurements can reliably establish, and whether those improvements survive a move from the scoreboard to the setting where somebody depends on them.

SCIENCE AND TECHNOLOGY: THE ECONOMY BENEATH OUR FEET

Quanta’s October 15 conversation with evolutionary biologist Toby Kiers follows nutrient exchange between plants and underground fungi. The episode describes decentralized networks that redirect resources without a brain, and tools that make those movements observable. Its full transcript is available alongside the roughly 57-minute recording. The attraction is a view of ecosystems as active exchanges rather than a collection of isolated organisms. But the language of bargains should not be mistaken for evidence of conscious intention.

Two primary research sources sharpen the account. A 2019 study, available through the University of Amsterdam repository, used differently colored fluorescent nanoparticles to track phosphorus through fungal networks. Under more unequal resource conditions, fungi transferred more phosphorus to roots, stored less, and moved nutrients from richer to poorer patches. That supports a specific experimental finding about resource allocation, not a claim that forests possess humanlike economic reasoning.

The research organization SPUN explains a separate global carbon estimate. A synthesis covering three major groups of mycorrhizal fungi estimated annual allocation equivalent to 13.12 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide. Critically, allocation is not permanent sequestration. SPUN explicitly notes that carbon also leaves these systems, including through respiration, and that the amount remaining underground needs further investigation. The scale of the flow is impressive; it cannot simply be deducted from annual human emissions.

Our interpretation is that this distinction between flows and durable stocks matters well beyond fungi. Imagine a reservoir receiving a large volume of water each year. Without knowing how much leaves, that inflow cannot tell us how much extra water the reservoir holds. In carbon accounting, the additional question is what would have happened without a proposed intervention. Protecting an existing ecosystem and creating a new, durable carbon removal are not interchangeable claims.

The broader contribution to a worldview issue is methodological humility. New instruments can reveal an enormous system before researchers fully understand how to manage it. It is reasonable to value that system before every function has been quantified; it is not reasonable to turn an uncertain mechanism into a precise climate credit. The productive middle ground is better observation, experiments that can distinguish rival explanations, and conservation arguments that do not depend on exaggerated promises. This is science with direct relevance to policy, but not a ready-made policy formula.

CULTURE AND IDEAS: WHAT MUST A TRADITION KEEP?

Wu Haiyun’s October 13 Sixth Tone interview with Yue opera artist Mao Weitao explores renewal from inside a Chinese performing tradition. Mao’s work invites controversy because it changes staging and storytelling while claiming continuity with the art form. She identifies dialect, the aesthetic of female performers playing male roles, and suggestive rather than literal expression as things worth retaining. The interview is an artist’s argument for her own practice, not an impartial verdict on every critic.

The official West Kowloon programme supplies independent production context. Su Dongpo was presented at Hong Kong’s Xiqu Centre in April 2026, with Mao as artistic director, He Jiping as playwright, and Roy Szeto as director. The programme describes cooperation between Zhejiang performers and a Hong Kong creative team. He explains that she rejected a chronological biography in favor of selected moments arranged into a dreamlike structure. The venue identifies Xiaobaihua as a troupe founded in 1984 with an all-female cast and a history of blending traditional and modern elements. These are useful records of the production and its creators’ intentions, although a house programme is promotional rather than independent criticism.

Our comparative question is whether preservation should protect an object, a technique, an audience relationship, or the capacity to create new work. Those goals can conflict. A museum can preserve a costume without sustaining a living community of performers. A commercially successful adaptation can attract newcomers while making experienced audiences feel that something essential has disappeared. Neither popularity nor longevity settles the argument on its own.

Consider the hypothetical case of a regional theatre switching entirely to a national language. It might become easier to market, but lose rhythmic possibilities that made its songs distinctive. Conversely, preserving every inherited stage convention might make the work inaccessible to people who no longer share its assumptions. The interesting task is to identify which constraints generate the art and which merely reflect an earlier production environment.

That is why this belongs beside economics and geopolitics. China is not only a state, a factory system, or a technology competitor. It is also a place where artists and audiences contest the meaning of inheritance. Listening to that disagreement broadens the frame without turning one celebrated performer into a representative of an entire society. It also offers a question for any institution confronting change: what are we preserving, and how would we know if it remained alive?

CULTURE AND SOCIETY: WHO DECIDES WHAT THE SEA TAKES?

Kat Hill’s October 15 Noema feature, What We Can Save In A Time Of Endings, considers coastal loss through places, communities, and memory. It contrasts the protection of hazardous infrastructure with the uncertain futures of homes and heritage. The central claim is ethical: deciding what to defend cannot be reduced to a detached inventory of assets. The piece is reported and reflective, not a comprehensive engineering assessment of every location it describes.

Official guidance provides the institutional background. England’s twenty Shoreline Management Plans set out approaches to flood and erosion risk through 2105. They distinguish holding an existing line, allowing a more natural coastline, moving the shoreline in a managed way, and extending defenses seaward. The Environment Agency says these choices depend on funding and can change as evidence changes. Plans also inform local development decisions; they are not simply construction instructions.

A government response to Parliament’s coastal-erosion inquiry acknowledges the human consequences: displacement, lost livelihoods, and effects on health and wellbeing. It describes consultation and revisions to planning policy, alongside its account of funding reforms. That response is evidence of the government’s stated approach, not proof that every affected household receives adequate support. The gap between a policy’s recognition of harm and its practical remedy is precisely where scrutiny belongs.

Our analysis is that adaptation contains two different distributive decisions. The first is where physical protection goes. The second is who pays when protection is not chosen. A technically sound decision to avoid defending a particular coast could still be socially unjust if a small community bears the entire loss. Conversely, defending every existing property regardless of cost or downstream effects is not automatically fair to people elsewhere.

Imagine two villages facing comparable erosion, one with expensive houses and one with modest homes. A calculation driven only by market property values could favor the wealthier place even if displacement would be harder for the poorer residents. That hypothetical does not describe a particular official decision; it exposes a question any appraisal should answer. Are we valuing buildings, people's ability to recover, or both?

The wider lesson is not that economics should be abandoned. It is that the quantities entering an economic decision already embody moral choices. A fuller account should distinguish irreplaceable memories from replaceable structures, explain uncertainty, and make responsibility for unavoidable losses visible. Climate adaptation is not only about managing water. It is about governing the transition between one way of living and another.

GLOBAL STRUCTURES: SUDAN IS NOT SIMPLY AN ABSENCE OF ORDER

Alden Young and Muez Ali’s October 20 Noema essay challenges the idea that Sudan’s devastation is only the disappearance of international order. They argue that older commercial and political geographies help explain the structures emerging amid state fragmentation. Their framework links infrastructure, export commodities, and the ability of central authorities to capture revenue. The historical analogy is an interpretation, not a prediction that the past will repeat unchanged.

A related research essay from Noria, Black Gold, Liquid Metal, adds a concrete mechanism and an important qualification. Artisanal gold is dispersed, portable, and can be remelted, making production and circulation harder to control through a few fixed facilities. Oil, by contrast, depends more visibly on fields and pipelines. Noria describes how taxation, checkpoints, commercial networks, and predation can extract value from gold without consolidating a single territorial state. Crucially, it contrasts Sudan with South Sudan, where gold has also supported incumbent power. The commodity creates opportunities, but does not determine one political outcome. The authors caution against reducing the regional conflict to a simple contest over a mineral.

Our analytical takeaway is to distinguish disorder for civilians from the absence of organization among armed and commercial actors. A system can be destructive, insecure, and illegitimate while still containing durable incentives and carefully maintained transactions. That is not an excuse for violence. It is a reason to ask more precise questions about how violence is financed and reproduced.

Imagine an armed organization that earns income at several border crossings rather than through one capital-city treasury. Capturing the capital might not end its capacity to operate. Equally, an agreement between recognized governments might leave the relevant traders and intermediaries untouched. This is a hypothetical illustration of the framework, not a claim that any single measure would resolve Sudan’s war.

For outside readers, the value is a shift in scale. Diplomacy is usually narrated through leaders and formal states. Commercial geography asks where goods move, who can interrupt those movements, who supplies credit, and which actors benefit from keeping authority fragmented. The danger is overextending the model until infrastructure explains everything. Political choices, social histories, and external involvement still matter. The essay is most useful as a corrective to a vacuum metaphor: when one order breaks down, analysts should look for the specific relationships taking its place, while keeping the suffering of civilians at the center of the account.

TECHNOLOGY AND DEVELOPMENT: OPTIMISM CAN BE A DIAGNOSIS OF EXCLUSION

Payal Arora’s October 15 Rest of World essay asks Western readers to take seriously the enthusiasm for AI found elsewhere. Her argument is that people excluded from established opportunities may see technology as a means of entry rather than chiefly a threat to what they already possess. This is an argument about perspective and possibility, not proof that every adoption produces a good outcome or that an entire region shares one attitude.

Stanford’s 2026 AI Index offers a useful check against a simple optimism-versus-fear story. Its public-opinion chapter reports that the global share seeing more benefits than drawbacks rose from 55 percent in 2024 to 59 percent in 2025. Yet 52 percent also said AI made them nervous. India recorded a fourteen-percentage-point increase in nervousness, much larger than its increase in excitement. Workplace use was high in several emerging economies, but use and approval are different measures. These findings support the possibility that enthusiasm and anxiety coexist. They do not establish that national attitudes cause particular economic results, and survey questions and populations must be compared carefully.

Our interpretation is that the appropriate baseline changes the evaluation. Imagine an aspiring entrepreneur who cannot afford professional translation. An imperfect tool might expand the set of customers she can reach. Now imagine an established translator whose clients substitute the same tool for paid work. The technology is identical; the relevant alternative, and the distribution of gains and losses, are not. These examples are illustrative, not people independently interviewed for this issue.

That does not settle the policy question in favor of either unrestricted deployment or prohibition. It suggests evaluating access, reliability, bargaining power, and recourse separately. A system that helps someone enter a market could still expose them to misleading output or dependence on a platform they cannot influence. Likewise, protecting an incumbent profession could preserve valuable expertise or unnecessarily exclude newcomers. Those possibilities require evidence, not an automatic verdict based on enthusiasm alone.

The complement to a business-centered account is attention to what users want institutions to do for them. Growth statistics can show adoption without explaining whether people feel more capable or more replaceable. The better question is not which population has the correct emotional response to AI. It is which barriers a particular application removes, which new dependencies it creates, and whether those affected have a meaningful say in the terms on which it enters their lives.


CLOSING PERSPECTIVE

Across these readings, the recurring question is what a headline leaves outside its frame. A national income ranking may leave out leisure. A change in trading currency may leave an old loan untouched. Stable investment totals can conceal a different ownership structure. A diplomatic agreement can leave status expectations unresolved. An impressive scientific measurement may describe a flow rather than a lasting stock. These are not reasons to abandon measurement or comparison. They are reasons to choose the measure that matches the question, and to make the remaining uncertainty visible.

The best counterweight is not an automatic opposing opinion. It is another well-supported way of seeing the same system, capable of changing what we would investigate next. This week’s selection includes researchers, policy analysts, artists and regional perspectives whose claims deserve scrutiny as well as attention. Take the strongest question from each, rather than treating the issue as a set of conclusions to memorize. Thank you for listening.
