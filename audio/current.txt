TITLE: Worldview Weekly — September 10, 2026

Worldview Weekly. September tenth, twenty twenty-six.

This week’s issue is organized around a recurring tension in global affairs: the difference between trying to control a system and understanding how the system adapts.

That tension appears in manufacturing policy, where governments can move tariffs, subsidies, and supply chains but cannot easily reverse the productivity forces reducing industrial employment. It appears in geopolitics, where sanctions can inflict severe economic pain without necessarily producing political capitulation, and where loose institutions such as BRICS can matter even without becoming coherent alliances. It appears in technology, where computing is becoming more distributed, more autonomous, and more infrastructural. And it appears in social life, where markets and technologies can move faster than the institutions meant to absorb them.

The twelve selections come from eight main publications, with perspectives originating in Taiwan, China, India, Australia, Europe, and the United States. Most were published in the last week. The reading links in the written feed have been chosen for practical accessibility. Foreign Affairs and The New Yorker remain on their publisher links because those sources have worked in Brave even when an embedded browser has put up a paywall. The Project Syndicate pieces use openly readable authorized syndication or author reposts where that reduces friction.

The goal is not to manufacture an ideological opposite to The Economist. It is to complicate the picture: to put firm-level evidence next to industrial strategy, Chinese and Indian strategic interpretations next to Western ones, institutional capacity next to market logic, and lived social effects next to aggregate measures.

Business and political economy.

The first three pieces are best read together. A great deal of current economic policy assumes that the geography of production can be deliberately reorganized. In some domains, it can. Governments can subsidize factories, restrict sensitive exports, cultivate domestic suppliers, and encourage firms to move activity toward politically aligned countries. But there are at least three different objectives hiding inside the word resilience: securing access to critical goods, increasing domestic production, and creating domestic employment. Those objectives overlap less than political rhetoric often implies.

A country can become more secure without creating many jobs. A company can reduce geopolitical exposure without bringing activity home. A local supplier can become strategically indispensable through capabilities accumulated over decades rather than through a single large subsidy. This section therefore looks below the headline categories of globalization and deglobalization. The useful unit of analysis is often the production system itself: capital intensity, supplier qualification, engineering know-how, client relationships, network structure, and the rate at which productivity changes the amount of labor required.

First: Adair Turner, writing for Project Syndicate, on China’s permanent manufacturing revolution.

Turner’s central claim is that China’s industrial advantage can no longer be reduced to direct subsidies. He points to low capital costs, rapid infrastructure provision, strong engineering capabilities, intense domestic competition, learning by doing, and very large production scale. The result is not simply that Chinese factories take market share. It is that they demonstrate how few workers increasingly are needed to produce large quantities of manufactured goods.

That distinction changes the labor-policy question. Tariffs or local-content rules may alter where factories are located. Chinese investment abroad may localize some production. But new plants built with highly automated processes and high-productivity equipment will not recreate the employment intensity of earlier industrialization. Turner’s argument is therefore less about whether governments should pursue industrial policy than about what industrial policy can plausibly accomplish. Security of supply and control of production capacity may remain meaningful goals even if mass factory employment becomes structurally harder to restore.

The deeper counterweight is to the idea that the next China shock is mainly a trade-policy problem. Turner treats it as a global productivity transition. If that is right, countries from Europe to India will face the same underlying employment arithmetic even under very different trade regimes.

Second: Taiwan’s CommonWealth Magazine, 天下雜誌, on Chiaoli Chemical and the fluorine supply chain behind advanced manufacturing.

The article follows a company that built capabilities in fluorine chemistry long before semiconductor resilience became a geopolitical slogan. Chiaoli moved from industrial chemicals into pharmaceutical-grade fluoride and then into semiconductor-grade hydrofluoric acid, where purity, process control, and customer qualification are unusually demanding. When Japanese export controls disrupted South Korean access to hydrogen fluoride in 2019, that accumulated Taiwanese capability suddenly became strategically valuable.

The important point is temporal. A critical supplier cannot usually be improvised after a crisis begins. Qualification takes time; customers need evidence; production knowledge is partly tacit; and reliability is built through repeated delivery. The most visible parts of the semiconductor industry are fabs, lithography tools, and advanced chips. This story instead shows the strategic importance of an input that is chemically mundane but operationally indispensable.

It is a useful case of industrial policy viewed from the middle of the supply chain rather than from the commanding heights. Resilience can depend on decades of technical specialization that barely registers in national industrial narratives until something breaks.

Third: Bruno Merlevede and Bernhard Michel at CEPR’s VoxEU on multinational restructuring, friendshoring, and what actually happens at home.

Using a large dataset of European multinational networks, the authors document a shift during the 2010s from foreign expansion toward more contraction, with nearshoring and friendshoring becoming increasingly common. The politically consequential result is what they do not find. When firms reduce foreign affiliates, domestic value added and employment do not rise in a compensating way. Contraction abroad is not the mirror image of expansion at home.

By contrast, firms expanding abroad often also expand activity in their home economies. That is consistent with a long literature in which foreign and domestic operations can be complements rather than substitutes. The authors are careful about causality: the data describe patterns around restructuring episodes, not a clean experiment proving that contraction causes weaker domestic activity.

The broader lesson is still useful. Supply-chain reorientation can reduce exposure to geopolitical risk without delivering reshoring in the popular sense. Governments and voters may therefore need to distinguish resilience outcomes from employment outcomes. A policy can achieve one without achieving the other.

Taken together, these three pieces make a fairly sharp proposition. The physical map of production is becoming more political, but the labor content of production is becoming more technological. Those two movements can happen at the same time. A factory can move closer and employ fewer people. A supplier can become strategically important because of knowledge accumulated over decades. A multinational can shrink its foreign network without rebuilding a domestic one.

That is a more difficult political economy than either free-trade nostalgia or simple reshoring narratives allow.

Global politics and institutions.

The next section asks a related question at the level of states: what counts as power when outcomes depend on adaptation rather than formal control?

Three common shortcuts are worth resisting. The first is to infer geopolitical alignment from summit photographs. States can cooperate institutionally while remaining competitors in other domains. The second is to infer successful coercion from economic damage. Pain is an input into political outcomes, not the outcome itself. The third is to assume that strategic confidence mechanically produces aggression. Sometimes confidence increases risk appetite; sometimes it gives leaders more room to tolerate limited competition without treating every dispute as existential.

These distinctions matter because the emerging international system is likely to contain more partial arrangements. Countries will participate in overlapping institutions. Economic relationships will survive political rivalry. Security competition may coexist with efforts to stabilize specific channels. The binary categories of ally and adversary remain useful in some contexts, but they explain less of the middle ground than they once did.

Fourth: Da Wei in Foreign Affairs, on what he calls the emerging U.S.-China détente.

Da, who directs Tsinghua University’s Center for International Security and Strategy, interprets the current bilateral framework of strategic stability as a meaningful change in how Beijing understands competition. His claim is modest in one sense: this is not partnership, and it does not resolve the major disputes between the two countries. It is an attempt to put a floor under the relationship so that competition does not repeatedly escalate into crisis.

The distinctive part of Da’s argument is his interpretation of Chinese confidence. He suggests that Beijing is now more willing to accept competition as a durable feature of the relationship because it is less fearful that competition itself will derail China’s development. In that reading, confidence can support restraint rather than necessarily produce greater assertiveness.

The caveat is important. U.S.-China relations have stabilized several times in recent years and then deteriorated again. Da therefore places weight on practical ballast: military communication, crisis-management mechanisms, controlled economic friction, and repeated high-level contact. The value of the piece is not that détente is guaranteed. It is that it gives a clear account of how an influential Chinese strategist understands the conditions under which managed competition might become sustainable.

Fifth: Brahma Chellaney, writing for Project Syndicate, on BRICS at twenty.

Chellaney argues that BRICS is often judged against the wrong benchmark. It has not become a monetary union, a single market, or a disciplined political alliance. Its members disagree on major strategic questions, and several are direct geopolitical rivals. But if the benchmark is whether emerging economies have built an additional platform for cooperation and strategic autonomy, he judges the record more consequential.

The grouping has expanded, created the New Development Bank, and pursued ways to settle more trade outside a single financial channel. None of that implies a unified anti-Western bloc, and Chellaney explicitly treats internal diversity as a constraint. The interesting institutional question is whether a loose coalition can remain useful precisely because it does not require ideological uniformity.

That framing is valuable because it avoids two symmetrical mistakes: treating BRICS as mostly ceremonial, or treating every BRICS initiative as evidence of a consolidated rival order. A heterogeneous institution can matter at the margin by widening members’ options without becoming a new NATO, European Union, or Bretton Woods system.

Sixth: Esfandyar Batmanghelidj in Foreign Affairs, on why economic pressure has not strangled Iran.

Batmanghelidj’s argument starts from a mismatch between macroeconomic damage and state capacity. Iran’s economy has suffered severe contraction, inflation, currency depreciation, sanctions, military strikes, and disruption to maritime trade. Those measures impose real costs. But he argues that years of adaptation have made the Iranian state better able to protect its core functions while transferring a large share of the burden to households and ordinary firms.

The mechanisms matter: inventories built under recurring sanctions, non-Western trade channels, exchange-rate adjustment, domestic substitution, and state control over scarce resources. War and blockade create pressures that sanctions alone did not, so resilience is not unlimited. But a state can become poorer without losing the specific resources required to resist external coercion.

The broader analytical point is to separate economic pain from political leverage. If the objective of coercion is behavioral change, macro indicators are incomplete evidence. The relevant question is how losses are distributed inside the target economy and whether the governing system can insulate the capabilities it values most.

These three pieces produce a useful composite picture of multipolarity. It is not simply a story of the West declining and a coherent alternative rising. It is a world of strategic floors, hedging institutions, partial autonomy, and states that learn to function inside pressure. BRICS can matter without being unified. U.S.-China competition can remain dangerous while becoming more managed. Iran can absorb enormous civilian economic damage while preserving state resistance.

That suggests a broader discipline for reading geopolitics: ask less often whether one side is winning in the abstract, and more often which mechanism is supposed to translate resources into the desired outcome.

Science and technology.

The two technology selections concern infrastructure at very different scales: one is mathematical and computational, the other literally orbital.

They share a more general theme. Technology changes not only what can be done but where verification, trust, and strategic dependence have to live. When autonomous systems contribute to a difficult mathematical proof, the bottleneck shifts toward problem definition, formalization, checking, attribution, and access to computational resources. When data processing moves from terrestrial data centers toward satellites, familiar cloud questions—ownership, standards, resilience, jurisdiction, physical vulnerability—move with it.

This is a useful way to think about technological transitions. The first-order capability attracts attention because it is easy to name: solve the theorem, put compute in orbit, generate the answer, deploy the model. The second-order institutions are usually slower. Who can reproduce the result? Who controls the infrastructure? What counts as independent verification? What happens when a system becomes critical before governance norms are mature?

Seventh: Quanta Magazine on an AI-assisted proposed solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem.

Quanta reports that an OpenAI-led effort used a very large population of autonomous agents running an unreleased model to construct a three-dimensional Navier–Stokes blowup, then formalized the proof in Lean. If mathematical review confirms that the formal statement matches the Millennium problem as intended, the result would be an extraordinary milestone for both fluid mathematics and AI-assisted theorem proving.

The story is stronger because it does not present the result as an isolated machine epiphany. The successful route builds on an analytic strategy developed by human mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, while a separate team involving Tristan Buckmaster and Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge was pursuing closely related results. Formal verification greatly reduces one class of error, but humans still have to confirm that the formal object corresponds to the right mathematical claim.

The event therefore illustrates both capability and dependence: frontier AI can search enormous mathematical spaces, but framing, prior theory, verification, credit, and access to expensive closed models remain central.

Eighth: the Lowy Institute on the cloud moving into orbit.

Nimra Javed starts from a concrete change: Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications says it has put an orbital-computing cloud into regular operation, linking satellite computing resources with ground stations and terrestrial data centers. The novelty is not that satellites contain processors. They always have. It is the attempt to expose computation in orbit as a reusable service.

Processing data nearer to where satellites generate it could reduce downlink requirements and shorten decision loops. It also offers a theoretical escape from some terrestrial constraints on land, cooling, water, and power, while U.S. companies are exploring related ideas.

But the cloud does not become geopolitically neutral by leaving Earth. Orbital compute would still depend on launch capacity, spectrum, inter-satellite links, standards, ground infrastructure, collision management, and protection against interference or attack. Javed’s useful move is to treat space not merely as a destination for satellites but as a possible layer of digital infrastructure. If that layer becomes economically important, cloud sovereignty becomes partly space policy.

Society, culture, and ideas.

The next two pieces are deliberately less abstract. They look at how societies design childhood and how a state tries to design cultural prestige.

Both are examples of social systems in which formal policy explains only part of what happens. Childhood is shaped by schools, streets, parental norms, transportation, liability fears, schedules, technology, and the informal expectations of other parents. A tourism economy is shaped by investment and regulation, but also by labor, taste, local legitimacy, repeat visitors, service quality, and whether an imported prestige model becomes embedded in everyday economic life.

This is where a global weekly benefits from moving away from cabinet rooms and balance sheets. Institutions are not only treaties and ministries. They also include the arrangements that determine whether a child can walk somewhere alone, whether a restaurant has a stable labor pool, whether local culture is treated as an asset or decoration, and whether people can participate in a system without constant expert supervision.

Ninth: Joshua Rothman in The New Yorker, asking whether modern societies have destroyed too much of childhood autonomy.

Rothman examines psychologist Peter Gray’s argument that the decline of unsupervised play and independent movement predates the smartphone era and may be important to understanding rising anxiety among young people. He sets that thesis against Jonathan Haidt’s emphasis on phones and social media, noting that the empirical literature on digital harms is more mixed than the public debate often suggests.

The value of the piece is the widened causal frame. If childhood has become more scheduled, supervised, academically managed, and physically constrained, then limiting devices addresses only one part of the environment in which children develop agency.

Tenth: Ruby Tandoh in The New Yorker, on Saudi Arabia’s pursuit of Michelin stardom.

Tandoh uses restaurants to examine Vision 2030 from ground level. Saudi institutions are funding destination dining, celebrity-chef projects, culinary education, and tourism development, especially around places such as AlUla, while also trying to elevate Saudi cuisine itself. The resulting sector has to solve ordinary but difficult problems: staffing, logistics, demand, service culture, migrant labor, and the gap between prestige events and a durable local restaurant ecosystem.

The broader value is methodological. Economic diversification looks different when measured through the daily operation of a restaurant rather than through announced capital spending. Cultural industries require repeated human execution, not only impressive physical assets.

Flexible selections: work, technology, and reproduction.

The final two pieces return to technological change but from the perspective of ordinary decisions made before the long-run consequences are known.

Technological diffusion is often described as though adoption follows invention automatically. In reality, diffusion requires people to translate capabilities into domain-specific practice. That translation work can itself become a labor market. Lawyers have to teach systems legal patterns, architects have to create realistic design tasks, clinicians have to define appropriate cases, and evaluators have to judge where an answer fails. The people performing that work may simultaneously benefit from the new market and worry that they are training a substitute for parts of their own expertise.

Reproductive technology creates a different version of the same timing problem. Markets can offer a service before the predictive science is mature, before professional consensus is stable, and before society has decided what distinctions it wants to make between avoiding serious disease and optimizing probabilistic traits. Once families are making individual decisions, governance is no longer debating a hypothetical future. It is responding to a market already in motion.

Eleventh: Rest of World on China’s white-collar experts becoming gig workers for AI training.

Viola Zhou reports on architects, lawyers, engineers, teachers, and other professionals taking side jobs that require them to create difficult work problems, supply examples, and evaluate model answers. This is a different labor market from the older image of low-paid generic data annotation. The scarce input is domain judgment.

The economic context is important. Some workers are taking these jobs because their primary industries are weak or their incomes have fallen. Tasks may take hours, pay only hundreds of yuan, and be rejected without compensation. At the same time, major Chinese technology companies are recruiting large pools of specialists because useful professional AI requires more than generic internet data.

The story complicates the standard automation narrative. Expertise is not simply replaced or preserved. It can be temporarily monetized as training material, creating a market in which workers are paid to encode precisely the judgment that future systems may perform more cheaply.

Twelfth: Noema on what it calls optimized offspring.

Brigida D’Agostino examines the expanding market around embryo testing and selection. Established reproductive genetics can identify some serious chromosomal or single-gene conditions. The more controversial frontier uses polygenic scores to rank embryos on complex outcomes influenced by many genes and by environment. Commercial services are moving into this space even though the American Society for Reproductive Medicine has said polygenic embryo screening remains unproven for clinical use.

The article is careful to distinguish embryo selection available today from direct gene editing, which raises a different level of technical and ethical uncertainty. The central social question is what happens when individual parental choice interacts with probabilistic prediction, wealth differences, commercial incentives, and cultural ideas about desirable traits.

The important point is not that a genetically optimized future is inevitable. It is that markets can create path dependence before predictive validity is settled. Once affluent families are offered a purchasable probability shift, decisions about fairness, medical benefit, consumer protection, and the boundary between treatment and enhancement become practical rather than speculative.

Closing synthesis.

Across these twelve pieces, four distinctions are worth carrying forward.

First, location is not employment. China’s manufacturing productivity, Taiwan’s specialized chemical suppliers, and European multinational restructuring all point toward a world in which the geography of production can change without restoring old labor patterns. Resilience, capacity, and jobs are separate outcomes. Any serious industrial analysis has to say which one it is measuring.

Second, damage is not leverage. Iran can suffer severe economic harm while preserving state capacity. A geopolitical institution can remain internally divided while still widening members’ options. Two strategic competitors can remain adversarial while building mechanisms that reduce the probability of accidental escalation. The observable headline—GDP loss, summit attendance, a bilateral statement—does not by itself tell us whether the underlying mechanism is working.

Third, capability is not self-validating. An AI-generated mathematical proof becomes historically important only if the mathematical object is the right one and the verification chain holds. Orbital computing becomes economically useful only if it can be operated, secured, connected, and governed. The social value of a technology usually depends on a layer of institutions that arrives after the technical demonstration.

Fourth, markets often move before consensus. Chinese professionals are already selling pieces of their expertise into AI training pipelines. Families can already purchase increasingly elaborate embryo screening services. Saudi Arabia is already spending to create a culinary destination whose durable demand is not yet guaranteed. Parents have already reorganized childhood around supervision and scheduling even while researchers continue to debate which changes drove which outcomes.

The common lesson is not pessimistic. It is methodological. When a system is changing quickly, look for the adaptation mechanism. Ask who absorbs the cost, which capability is actually scarce, what is being measured, what has to be verified, and which institution is carrying the transition. That usually tells you more than the slogan attached to the policy or technology.
