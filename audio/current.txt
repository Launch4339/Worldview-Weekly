TITLE: Worldview Weekly — October 8, 2026

Worldview Weekly, for October eighth, twenty twenty-six.

This issue follows a deceptively simple question: when a government, company or institution promises to remove a risk, has it actually removed it, or merely moved the bill somewhere less visible?

Taiwan has sheltered households from energy-price increases, but its public energy companies and treasury now carry more of the burden. AI companies are buying computing power, while phone buyers in poorer countries encounter a more expensive memory-chip market. In the Pacific, foreign aid meant to secure alliances may reinforce domestic patronage. On the internet, thousands of miles of cables can create an illusion of redundancy if they share one dangerous stretch of sea. Even the evidence itself can be misleading: a decline relative to an employment trend is not the same as layoffs, and an electrical wave in the brain is not yet proof that the wave causes thought.

Our twelve readings come from seven publications. They range from macroeconomics and African security to Korean construction finance, fluid turbulence, and neuroscience. Most are from the past week; a couple were worth bringing forward because they sharpen the larger story. The links and short summaries are in the written Worldview Weekly feed. What follows is a deeper, original synthesis, not a substitute for the authors' reporting.

We begin with business and political economy.

First, from CEPR and VoxEU: Not all geopolitical shocks are inflationary, by economist Marco Pinchetti.

The word geopolitical often works as a mental shortcut in economic commentary. A war starts, tensions rise, insurance becomes more expensive, and the instinct is to expect higher inflation. That instinct was reinforced by the energy shock following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. But this week's article makes an essential distinction: geopolitical danger can reach the economy through both supply and demand, and the price consequences can point in opposite directions.

Picture two events. In the first, fighting threatens an oil-producing region or shipping lane. Traders anticipate lost supply. Oil prices rise even if no production has yet stopped, because the probability of a shortage is enough to change contracts and inventories. Firms that use energy pay more, and households face higher bills. Output suffers even while prices climb. Economists call this an adverse supply shock.

In the second event, a crisis frightens investors and businesses so badly that they cancel projects, postpone travel and purchases, and revise growth expectations downward. Oil prices may fall because expected demand for energy declines. Output again suffers, but this time inflationary pressure falls with it. That is closer to a demand or uncertainty shock.

Pinchetti tries to separate these mechanisms empirically instead of categorizing whole wars as if each contained one single shock. His starting point is a geopolitical risk index based on news coverage, paired with oil-market movements around major events since the nineteen eighties. He looks at the joint movement in a short, three-day window. When geopolitical risk rises alongside oil, the evidence points more toward an energy-supply channel. When risk rises and oil falls, it suggests a broader deterioration in anticipated demand. Those signals then help identify shocks in a structural macroeconomic model.

The estimates are modest in size but stark in direction. An energy-type shock depresses industrial production and raises consumer prices by around two-tenths of one percent at its peak. A demand-like geopolitical shock also weakens activity, but the estimated consumer-price response is negative, with declines of roughly one-tenth to four-tenths of a percent across horizons.

There are limits. Oil prices are not a perfect diagnostic instrument. Speculation, exchange rates, inventories and policy responses can affect them simultaneously. Three-day market reactions and a structural model do not give us an experimental partition of a complex event. And these are estimated responses to standardized shocks, not predictions for any particular new conflict. The contribution is conceptual and empirical: pooling all geopolitical episodes into an average conceals mechanisms that may cancel each other in the inflation data.

The practical implication is for central banks and governments. If prices are rising because energy supply has been impaired, raising interest rates will not create more oil, though it may prevent inflation from spreading into longer-term expectations. If the dominant channel is collapsing demand, aggressive tightening could intensify the contraction. The right diagnosis requires watching actual transmission channels, not simply measuring how alarming the news sounds. Keep this distinction in mind when we reach Taiwan's energy dilemma and the physical bottleneck in the Red Sea.

Second, also from CEPR and VoxEU: The adjustment of the Danish labour market to AI has begun.

The public debate over AI and jobs often pits two observations against each other. On one side, capabilities in writing, coding and analysis are improving quickly. On the other, national unemployment statistics do not show mass displacement attributable to AI. Both observations can be true if the early effect is not a wave of dismissals but a quiet reduction in hiring.

Three researchers, Simone Maria Bonin, Saman Darougheh and Andreas Kuchler, use Danish administrative records to look beneath the aggregate numbers. They link official firm surveys recording adoption of AI in twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four to monthly employer-employee data. That allows them to study employment paths at companies that report adopting the technology, and to compare those paths with firms that do not report adoption, accounting for earlier trends and industry timing.

Their headline result needs particularly careful wording. By September twenty twenty-five, companies that had adopted AI in twenty twenty-three had employment about nine percent below the path implied by their own earlier trend, relative to nonadopters. That does not mean nine percent of employees were laid off. Many of these companies were still growing. The point is that growth was slower than the earlier trajectory suggested.

Where did the difference arise? The researchers find much of it in recruitment. The stock of recently hired employees falls relative to trend while longer-tenured employees are retained. Young university graduates working in occupations with high AI exposure appear especially affected. Those occupations were about twenty-seven percent below their prior employment trend in the comparison, compared with roughly twelve percent for other roles. Among firms with fewer than one hundred workers, the overall gap was about sixteen percent. Larger adopting firms did not show a statistically significant decline. A separate cohort of firms first adopting in twenty twenty-four followed a similar delayed pattern, which makes the timing harder to dismiss as one peculiar event in twenty twenty-three.

What explains the small-firm concentration? One possibility is that AI lets a small team accomplish more without proportionally expanding its staff. But the authors also note that capital constraints may matter: smaller firms that spend scarce resources on implementing new technology may have less left for payroll, at least initially. Their ongoing work finds a concentration among already leveraged businesses. A company can buy software, reorganize processes, and delay hiring without the software itself having causally replaced an employee. The study is observational. Early adopters may differ from nonadopters in ambition, business prospects, financing conditions or management quality. Its estimates should not be recited as a controlled causal effect of AI.

Still, the implication is consequential. Labor-market adjustment can be intergenerational. The person who has a job today may keep it, while the graduate who would have entered that occupation next year finds fewer openings. Over time, fewer junior positions can weaken the pipeline through which workers acquire judgment, tacit knowledge and promotion opportunities. That creates a different set of policy questions from the standard debate about retraining the unemployed. We may need to examine entry-level hiring, apprenticeships and professional development even when layoffs remain subdued.

The Danish evidence is early and covers a limited slice of the economy. It may or may not generalize to labor markets with different protections and business models. But it is a better starting question than asking only whether an AI unemployment crisis has appeared. Look at the hiring margin, not only the stock of jobs.

Third, from Taiwan's CommonWealth Magazine: Who Pays to Keep Taiwan's Energy Prices Down?

Think of Taiwan as an economy trying to satisfy three objectives at once: cushion households against global fuel-price shocks, remain competitive in energy-intensive semiconductor production, and maintain reliable imported-energy supplies. Each objective is defensible. It is their combination that becomes expensive.

CommonWealth traces the bill through the accounts of CPC, Taiwan's state-owned fuel company, and the government's new budget proposal. On October first, the administration proposed close to four hundred fifteen billion New Taiwan dollars in energy-related support. Roughly one hundred eighty-one billion would compensate energy suppliers for costs they absorbed under price-stabilization policies. Another roughly two hundred thirty-four billion would go into CPC as fresh capital to stabilize its finances and support infrastructure. Those are not the same financial operation. A subsidy covers an expense; a capital injection rebuilds a balance sheet. Both ultimately rely on public resources.

CPC has recorded losses for six consecutive years. The government estimates accumulated losses could approach one hundred twenty-eight billion New Taiwan dollars by the end of twenty twenty-six, close to CPC's existing paid-in capital. The company has disputed headlines predicting its bankruptcy next year. That distinction matters: a state-backed enterprise with an explicit rescue plan is not the same as a private business about to shut its doors. What is established is the deterioration and the need for government support, not a guaranteed corporate collapse.

Why the losses? Taiwan has repeatedly prevented higher world oil and natural-gas costs from appearing immediately in domestic prices. The article counts twenty-one price freezes for gasoline and diesel this year, additional limits on increases, and frozen household gas charges. This protects purchasing power and restrains measured inflation in the short run. It does not repeal the international price. The uncollected revenue or uncompensated cost sits first with the supplier, then potentially with taxpayers, future consumers or other public spending priorities.

Taiwan's semiconductor success complicates the arithmetic. From twenty nineteen to twenty twenty-five, electricity consumption in electronics manufacturing rose by thirty-five percent. Demand from factories and advanced packaging is rising even as Taiwan remains heavily dependent on imported fuel. In recent years, weaker electricity demand from nontechnology industries offset part of the increase. That is not a permanent energy strategy.

There is no effortless policy alternative. Letting prices jump overnight can hurt lower-income households, trigger politically destabilizing inflation and burden productive companies. Subsidies can sensibly distribute a temporary shock over time. But a temporary buffer becomes a structural problem when the state company is expected to absorb repeated crises indefinitely. Targeted support, investment in supply resilience, gradual price signals and transparent fiscal accounting can be combined in different ways. Those choices have distributional consequences; none makes the resource cost vanish.

This story also changes how to think about industrial policy. Taiwan's manufacturing advantage is not only about excellent engineers, chipmaking equipment or geopolitical alliances. It rests on grid capacity, imported natural gas, regulated prices and the creditworthiness of a public enterprise. Anyone analyzing the future of advanced chips while ignoring those foundations is analyzing only the visible tip of the system.

Global politics and institutions.

Fourth, from The New Yorker, Ishaan Tharoor on why the fighting in Ethiopia belongs to a larger regional conflict.

It is easy for coverage of an African civil war to stop at a country's border. There is a capital, a government, a rebel force, a ceasefire and perhaps an international mediator. In Ethiopia, that tidy structure now obscures as much as it explains.

The new fighting follows the collapse of a peace that had ended the devastating Tigray war in twenty twenty-two. The prior conflict took an enormous civilian toll, though estimates of deaths remain uncertain and difficult to verify. Tensions among Ethiopia's central government, the Tigray People's Liberation Front and other armed actors never truly disappeared. But what makes the current violence especially dangerous is that the alignment of neighboring countries and outside backers has changed.

Eritrea illustrates the problem. Its soldiers once fought alongside Ethiopia's central government against Tigrayan forces. It now appears to be on the opposite side of that contest. Ethiopia and Eritrea have broken diplomatic relations, while Ethiopia's leadership has accused Egypt and Sudanese actors of fomenting rebellion. The accused governments deny those charges. Sudan's own civil war involves two major armed camps, the regular army and the Rapid Support Forces. Reporting about assistance and cross-border collaboration is contested in places, so we should distinguish documented diplomatic positions from allegations about covert sponsorship.

Even if we strip out disputed intelligence, three structural sources of tension remain. First, Ethiopia is landlocked and wants more reliable access to the Red Sea. Its attempts to establish ties with Somaliland alarm Somalia, which considers Somaliland part of its sovereign territory. Second, Egypt and Ethiopia are divided over water and the implications of Ethiopia's large dam on the Nile. Third, the Red Sea and the Horn attract outside security interests from Gulf states, Turkey and other powers, with ports, bases, trade routes and resources at stake.

The result is not one coordinated conspiracy. It is a set of overlapping security dilemmas. A decision taken in Addis Ababa can alarm Cairo. A port proposal can reshape calculations in Mogadishu. An alliance in the Sudanese war can affect a neighboring insurgency. When the parties are fighting on different maps, a ceasefire drawn on only one of those maps may not last.

This is where the article's international-order argument has force. Institutions are supposed to offer negotiation, humanitarian access and enforceable settlements. But if regional patrons calculate that they gain more from sustaining an ally than from imposing restraint, institutions struggle to change incentives. External military support need not be omnipotent to be destabilizing. It only has to reduce the cost to a participant of continuing the fight.

An alternative reading should also be heard. These conflicts have deep local histories; reducing them to a proxy war of foreign powers would deny the agency of Ethiopians, Sudanese, Eritreans and Somalis, including civilians working for peace. Nor is every new port agreement evidence of war planning. What matters is recognizing that internal and external causes operate together. A durable settlement would have to make several governments see a reason to restrain their allies, respect each other's core interests and allow people to rebuild. That is harder than brokering a photo opportunity between two negotiating teams.

Fifth, from the Lowy Institute, Mohd Yunus on dying coral reefs in the South China Sea.

Most discussion of the South China Sea is organized around control: which country occupies which reef, whether a ship has crossed a line, what an arbitral ruling says, and how maritime power is distributed. The Lowy essay adds a reality that does not care about those lines. Coral reefs, fish stocks, larvae, sediment and water quality interact across the contested maritime space.

China's ecological assessment in August reported that coral cover around Sandy Cay had fallen by almost seventy percent compared with twenty sixteen. China and the Philippines have each accused the other of damaging the environment. Large-scale Chinese reclamation earlier in the decade transformed submerged reefs into artificial islands with military facilities; Vietnam and the Philippines have also carried out reclamation in areas they control. Sediment from dredging can damage habitats beyond the immediate project. A twenty sixteen arbitration held that China had breached obligations to protect marine ecosystems, while China rejected the award and its jurisdiction.

The essay's central observation is institutional. Scientific reports are increasingly written or deployed as evidence of national innocence or an opponent's guilt. That does not mean the scientific data are worthless. It means incentives around their collection and use are skewed toward litigation and political messaging rather than a shared account of environmental decline. Governments argue about who owns a reef while the reef continues to deteriorate.

Yunus proposes an intentionally modest alternative. Strengthen joint ecological surveys between ASEAN and China, involve technically credible independent institutions, standardize sampling and reporting, and maintain shared data on coral, water and fisheries. Most importantly, participants would join under a without-prejudice clause: collecting samples together, exchanging data or entering a site would neither acknowledge nor surrender any sovereign claim.

This is a useful idea because it tries to separate an urgent technical problem from a dispute too entrenched for easy settlement. Fisheries and ecosystems provide a form of common interest without requiring a fictional shared political identity. But the hard part is enforcement. Who chooses survey sites? Who verifies that militaries and dredging operations obey environmental restrictions? Can independent scientists gain safe access to all relevant locations? Will states publish unfavorable results? A technical body needs lasting funding, audit rights and enough autonomy to withstand the next diplomatic confrontation.

The larger lesson is about making progress under disagreement. Cooperation can be designed not to settle the underlying conflict, but to prevent a third party—in this case an ecosystem—from paying the full cost while people argue. A country need not change its sovereignty position to agree on monitoring coral mortality. Whether powerful claimants will accept a credible common record is the political test.

Sixth, also from Lowy's Interpreter, Mary-Louise O'Callaghan on Australia's proposed funding for Solomon Islands legislators.

The strategic backdrop is familiar. Australia worries about China's growing security influence in the Pacific. Solomon Islands leaders need infrastructure, jobs and services. Canberra wants long-term security cooperation, and development finance can help bring partners together. On paper, diplomacy and assistance may reinforce each other.

O'Callaghan examines a reported arrangement that reveals how easily they can instead work at cross-purposes. A diplomatic message verified and published by Australian media reportedly described a plan to make about ninety-three million Australian dollars available through the constituencies of Solomon Islands' fifty members of Parliament. The plan would be part of a much larger package connected with security treaty negotiations. Neither government had officially confirmed all the leaked details when the analysis was published. This remains a reported proposal, not an enacted program whose effects can be measured.

Why is funding through lawmakers different from building a road through an independently audited procurement system? Because each member could become the gatekeeper for benefits in their electorate. Constituency projects may certainly meet real needs, and locally chosen investments can be appropriate. But in a political system where patronage already shapes power, discretionary resources can reward personal loyalty rather than broad public benefit. Aid can become a mechanism for keeping coalition members in office, even if the donor's stated goal is institutional development.

There is also a democratic legitimacy problem. If a foreign country offers resources to politically influential actors while bargaining over security commitments, opponents may plausibly characterize the money as buying a strategic alignment. That accusation can weaken the very alliance it is meant to secure. The short-term reward of securing an agreement may come at the long-term cost of mistrust and weaker public institutions.

A realist might respond that the alternative is worse. If Australia refuses to offer useful resources, rival powers may step in, and Solomon Islands citizens could receive less investment and less security cooperation. That argument cannot be dismissed with a generic appeal to good governance. But it does not establish that every delivery channel is equally effective. The relevant comparison is not aid versus no aid; it is whether aid could be structured to provide visible constituency benefits under transparent eligibility rules, public accounting and independent review, rather than reinforcing individual patrons.

The case illustrates a recurrent trap in foreign policy. The donor treats the partner country as a location on a strategic map; citizens experience the bargain through their political institutions. A program that looks like leverage from Canberra may look like favoritism in Honiara. If a security agreement is to last across elections and leadership changes, its legitimacy inside the smaller country is not an optional afterthought. It is part of its strategic value.

The next section moves from institutions to science. Two stories challenge apparently simple descriptions of how complex systems behave.

Science and technology.

Seventh, Quanta Magazine on sea monkeys and the rules of turbulence.

Brine shrimp, tiny creatures once sold as sea monkeys, provide an unexpectedly powerful way to study a deep question in fluid mechanics. When a fluid becomes turbulent, how does energy move from structures of one size to those of another?

Think about stirring a mug of water. In many three-dimensional flows, energy entering through a large motion passes through progressively smaller eddies until viscosity dissipates it as heat. In approximately two-dimensional turbulence, the pattern can differ: energy often moves toward larger structures instead. Physics textbooks describe these contrasting cascades as a consequence of the dynamics and conservation laws of different-dimensional flows.

A University of Pittsburgh team used tiny actively swimming shrimp to inject disturbances into a thin layer of fluid. The collective movements created a laboratory version of active turbulence. The scientists introduced a small obstacle, then changed its angle relative to the flow. With the right orientation they could alter, and even reverse, the direction of energy transfer. It was not enough to know that the experiment was approximately two-dimensional. The placement and shape of a boundary also mattered.

This does not overthrow the underlying mathematics of turbulence. It clarifies which assumptions are necessary before the familiar result follows. Active swimmers, local forcing and physical boundaries can make a textbook generalization inadequate as a description of a particular experiment. The laboratory is small precisely so researchers can change one feature at a time and see the consequences.

There are obvious limits to what has been shown. An aquarium-scale experiment does not establish that the same simple intervention will control an ocean current or an industrial processing line. Suggestions about pollution dispersion and mixing are research possibilities, not proven applications. But the experiment identifies a potential mechanism worth testing at other scales.

The broader intellectual lesson is about boundary conditions. Sometimes the most productive question is not whether a scientific rule is true in the abstract, but what physical arrangements make it true. When an observation reverses under a small change in geometry, a theoretical distinction that once seemed sufficient must be made more precise. That is how a surprising experiment can strengthen a field rather than simply undermine its established principles.

Eighth, another Quanta report, on electrical waves in the brain.

The brain coordinates vast numbers of neurons, but there is an unresolved disagreement over the status of certain collective patterns. Are traveling waves of electrical activity part of the machinery that organizes thought? Or do those waves mainly reveal the underlying neuron-to-neuron computation without independently driving it?

Researchers used to describe relatively simple bands of activity moving across the cortex. Newer recordings can reveal much more intricate forms. Some patients with severe epilepsy have electrodes implanted for medical diagnosis. With appropriate research participation, those recordings can reveal activity across regions of cortex while people carry out memory tasks. Other experiments use laboratory mice.

The observed patterns include spirals, waves radiating outward from a source, and patterns converging on a region. In one human study, rotating waves appeared relatively often during a challenging spatial-memory task; simpler patterns were more common during verbal-memory exercises. Mouse experiments have found coordinated rotation across the hemispheres and circuits whose geometry may support these patterns.

One hypothesis is that the waves provide a rapid layer of organization. Anatomical connections change relatively slowly, but a brain must move between tasks in seconds. Traveling electrical activity might change the excitability and timing of different areas, coordinating information flow without physically rewiring the network.

The competing account is important. Some neuroscientists argue that local electrical fields mostly reflect synaptic currents and coordinated firing. Under that model the neurons perform the computations while the wave patterns are useful signatures. Observing a spiral alongside spatial memory does not show that disrupting the spiral would disrupt memory. And electrodes in epilepsy patients sample locations chosen for medical reasons; they may not represent the whole cortex.

This is a distinction between association and causation. A thermometer tracks a weather system without causing it, while in other physical systems a field can feed back and influence its components. To decide which relationship applies, scientists need interventions, predictive theories and evidence that separates the hypotheses. A compelling visualization is a beginning, not an answer.

Quanta's account is valuable precisely because it does not turn a beautiful pattern into a promise to decode thoughts. It shows researchers disagreeing about a clear causal question, and it explains what additional evidence would actually advance that disagreement.

Society, culture and ideas.

Ninth, from Taiwan's CommonWealth Magazine, an essay on why Asian firms have pursued risky overseas projects, and why their next challenge may be different.

David Kim traces the history of South Korean engineering companies taking on work where established competitors hesitated. The familiar account emphasizes tenacity, ambition and a willingness to take chances. He argues that this misses the machinery that made persistence possible: export credit, political-risk insurance, government diplomacy, large corporate balance sheets and operating expertise. A project that would be intolerably risky for a standalone contractor could become financeable when those institutions divided the exposure.

Korean companies expanded across the Middle East, sometimes in war zones, while Taiwan pursued overseas petroleum investments despite having relatively few diplomatic allies. Some Taiwanese ventures remained active even after host governments switched diplomatic recognition to Beijing. China later scaled state-backed infrastructure investment through its Belt and Road program. These histories differ, but they share an understanding of long-duration commitment as a competitive capability.

Yet the author does not present a success story without costs. Contractors lost money, geopolitical changes rewrote partnerships and projects sometimes created uncomfortable relationships with governments. The distinctive argument comes in his discussion of a Himalayan hydropower disaster in Nepal in twenty twenty-six. The project reportedly had development-bank participation, insurance and engineering safeguards. Nevertheless, a destructive collapse involving mountain and glacier hazards exceeded important planning assumptions. An insurance design centered on earthquake measurements is not automatically a hedge against a glacier-related slope failure and flood. The article is an analytical opinion essay rather than a formal accident investigation, so the exact attribution of responsibility should be treated as provisional.

Political risk usually involves actors who can change behavior, accept concessions or respond to diplomacy. Physical hazards obey no negotiating table. Worse, their frequency may change as glaciers retreat and climatic conditions depart from the historical record used in engineering models. A project can have sophisticated financing and still carry a badly measured exposure.

The policy dilemma is more complicated than simply raising premiums. If lenders fully price uncertain future hazards and withdraw, poorer countries may lose needed power, transport and connectivity. Ignoring risks, however, leaves families and taxpayers paying later. Better hazard mapping, adaptable engineering, transparent contingency planning and public guarantees may have to work together.

This piece moves industrial policy away from a simple contest of national courage. It asks how financial institutions decide what a risk is, who bears it, and whether a system built to handle politics can learn to handle rapidly changing physical conditions. The lesson also connects to our South China Sea reading: reliable science needs a role before a crisis, not just after competing interests begin assigning blame.

Tenth, from Noema, Nathan Gardels revisits Germany's reunification and the political meaning of belonging.

Economic explanations for nationalism commonly emphasize wage losses, regional decline, unemployment and inequality. Those are substantial factors. Gardels asks what happens when people also lose familiar institutions, shared routines and a sense that they belong to a recognizable social world.

He revisits conversations from the early nineteen nineties, shortly after the collapse of communist East Germany. Thinkers including Heiner Müller and Daniel Cohn-Bendit warned that reunification's immense expansion of freedom would coexist with dislocation and disappointed expectations. Old jobs and institutions disappeared. Some eastern families expected prosperity and social status to converge with the west far faster than proved possible. Intellectuals feared that a vacuum of identity and trust would create opportunities for xenophobic movements.

Gardels sees those warnings as relevant to the present strength of radical nationalism in parts of eastern Germany. His argument is that a liberal society needs more than formal rights and economic efficiency. It also needs a civic sense of belonging, credible integration of newcomers and immigration rules the public can regard as legitimate.

There are serious objections to his diagnosis. Interviews from three decades ago cannot establish a causal model of contemporary voting. Economic conditions, political organization, propaganda and opportunistic leadership also matter. National attachment can be healthy, but the same language can be used to exclude citizens or newcomers on grounds that undermine equal rights. Acknowledging these distinctions is essential.

Nor does the diagnosis dictate one immigration policy. Some will argue that clearer border enforcement creates space for sustainable openness. Others will argue that education, housing, employment, integration services and stronger protections against discrimination are more directly effective in rebuilding trust. Both positions require evidence about actual outcomes, not only persuasive stories about national identity.

The article is worth reading as a historically informed interpretation, not as a settled finding. It adds a time dimension to present political disputes. An institutional transformation may be declared complete by leaders, investors and constitutional lawyers while the social experience of transformation remains unsettled for a generation. Policy can be more effective when it recognizes that people need both material opportunity and a meaningful place in a shared civic community.

Our final two readings connect those hidden costs back to technology.

Two connections worth making.

Eleventh, Rest of World on a side effect of the AI boom that investors rarely discuss: the disappearance of the cheapest smartphones.

We usually think of the AI investment cycle in terms of data centers, electricity, chips, expensive networks and the productivity of people using AI services. But the same semiconductor industry supplies components to many other markets, and those components are not immune to competition for manufacturing capacity.

Reporter Kinling Lo follows the memory-chip market, where major suppliers have shifted resources toward the higher-value needs of AI infrastructure. The immediate consequence is pressure on the cost of low-priced mobile phones. Research quoted in the article suggests that existing phone models became roughly fifteen percent more expensive globally this year, and newly launched models even more. The burden is sharply unequal. Prices rose much faster in India and parts of Asia and Africa than in the United States, where wealthier buyers and higher-margin products dominate.

Manufacturers face a commercial choice. They can absorb higher memory prices in products that already have very thin margins, or they can move customers toward more expensive models and abandon the least profitable entry-level devices. Chinese manufacturers that once expanded access by offering increasingly capable low-cost phones have incentives to do the latter.

The results are large enough to matter socially. Rest of World cites industry figures showing that shipments of phones below one hundred dollars fell almost sixty percent year over year in the second quarter. In twenty twenty-five, more than a quarter of global smartphones shipped had cost less than one hundred fifty dollars. For many first-time buyers, the key issue is not a preference for premium features. A phone is the gateway to schooling, employment opportunities, banking, government services and communication with relatives.

The article cites a striking measure of affordability: for poorer households in sub-Saharan Africa, the cost of an entry-level phone can consume a very large fraction of monthly income. When that price rises, the alternatives may be borrowing a device, postponing replacement, relying on an old feature phone or going without mobile internet. All of those can carry real opportunity costs.

There are alternative pressures on phone prices too: currencies, tariffs, local taxes, model changes and distribution costs. It would be too strong to attribute every increase solely to data-center demand. But the common memory market gives a credible mechanism by which spending by the richest technology companies can make basic connectivity less affordable to the poorest users.

That is the counterweight here. Economic analysis of AI often measures the return on new computing capacity. Distributional analysis should also measure what that capacity displaces elsewhere. An innovation boom can be a tax on another part of the supply chain, even if no one intended the transfer.

Twelfth, also from Rest of World, on Red Sea conflict and the routes carrying the internet.

The image of the cloud suggests software floating above geography. In reality, moving data between Europe and Asia depends heavily on fiber-optic cables laid on the sea floor, passing through narrow straits and landing in politically vulnerable places.

The report says more than ninety percent of Europe-to-Asia data capacity passes through cables in the Red Sea. Near Yemen, the Bab al-Mandab strait compresses many routes into a relatively small space. One undersea cable can be backed up by another on a network diagram while the two remain exposed to the same regional conflict and the same difficulties in getting repairs done.

Rest of World reports that Google, Meta and other large companies have been seeking costly terrestrial alternatives. A person familiar with arrangements described purchases involving fiber along Turkish pipelines and the use of routes crossing Iraq. The specific claims about Google and Meta were not confirmed by those companies before publication, so they should be treated as sourced reporting, not company announcements. Microsoft, by contrast, publicly committed more than four hundred million dollars to regional connectivity investments through twenty thirty.

A key operational detail is easy to miss. A severed cable can sometimes be repaired in a matter of weeks once a specialist ship is available and permitted to work. In a war zone, the harder part may be obtaining access, insurance and assurances that a repair crew will not be attacked. Damage to cables in the region during earlier hostilities took months to resolve. Paying for nominally redundant capacity does little good if every route runs through the same danger zone.

Land-based routes are not a frictionless solution either. They cross jurisdictions, require right-of-way agreements and can be expensive. Some will serve mostly as standby capacity rather than carry routine traffic. That may still be rational insurance, but it means resilience has an ongoing cost that will show up in infrastructure investment, network pricing or customer service commitments.

The article turns geopolitics into something concrete for any household or enterprise using remote computing. A dispute over ports and waterways can affect payment apps, cloud services, global business transactions and the speed or reliability of international connections. Redundancy must be measured by independence of failure modes, not by counting cables.

And this brings us back to the beginning. Energy subsidies, employment trends, Pacific aid, offshore engineering, affordable phones and internet cables look like different stories. They share a discipline of inquiry. Ask where the underlying cost went. Ask whether a statistic measures what the headline implies. Ask which institution is responsible for a problem that crosses boundaries. And ask whether two arrangements that seem diverse on paper actually depend on the same fragile assumption.

No single publication or ideology has a monopoly on those questions. This week's authors disagree in methods, ambitions and politics. Their collective value lies in making important mechanisms visible, including the places where evidence is incomplete.
