TITLE: Worldview Weekly — September 24, 2026

Worldview Weekly for September twenty-fourth, twenty twenty-six.

This week’s issue is about systems that look simple from far away and become much more complicated once we ask where the costs, incentives, and adaptation actually sit.

A tariff is not only a tax on an import. It changes supplier relationships across several countries at once. An AI data center is not only a building full of servers. It is also a new electricity customer, a land user, a water user, and sometimes a political constituency. A geopolitical bloc is not necessarily an alliance, but it can still create options for its members. A new scientific idea does not have to carry the mechanism of its original field with it; sometimes the mathematics is what travels. And a technology boom is not merely a contest among laboratories. It becomes a labor market, a health system, a local infrastructure dispute, and a question about who gets to define acceptable risk.

That is the thread through the twelve pieces this week: follow the mechanism, not the slogan.

We begin with business and economics.

First, CEPR and VoxEU on how the U.S.-China trade war reshaped global production.

A common way to describe trade conflict is to look at bilateral imports and exports: one country buys less from another, and some third country gains the diverted trade. The research summarized here argues that this misses much of the adjustment. The authors use plant-level evidence across fifty economies and find that the trade war propagated through production networks. Firms in the middle of value chains could be hit from both directions, paying more for inputs while selling into customers that were themselves under pressure. Upstream and downstream activities sometimes benefited instead.

The broader lesson is that the object moving through the world economy is not a finished good with a single national identity. It is a chain of intermediate goods, affiliated plants, supplier relationships, financing arrangements, and sunk investments. A tariff aimed at one bilateral flow can therefore induce changes far outside the two countries named in the policy.

That matters for industrial policy. If policymakers ask only whether imports from China fell, they can miss whether the underlying production network became more resilient, more expensive, more concentrated, or simply rerouted. It also matters for claims about decoupling. A supply chain can become less directly Chinese without becoming less dependent on Chinese firms, Chinese inputs, or production know-how.

Second, CEPR and VoxEU on data centers and electricity prices.

The AI boom is usually narrated through models, chips, investment, and market capitalization. But the physical layer is increasingly important. Data centers consume large amounts of electricity, and the new facilities are geographically concentrated. The authors examine utility service territories in the United States and find evidence that rapid data-center growth can raise retail electricity prices, especially for households. The effect is much smaller in territories served by nonprofit utilities.

The interesting point is not merely that large new loads can push up demand. It is that the distribution of costs depends on the institutions governing the grid. A utility with spare capacity may absorb a new customer cheaply. A constrained system may need new generation or network investment. Regulators then decide which customers bear those costs.

So one useful way to think about the economics of AI is to separate the private value of computation from the public infrastructure required to supply it. A company may capture the upside from a new data center while some of the grid costs are socialized. That does not make data centers bad investments. It means that the incidence of the investment is partly a regulatory question.

Third, the Lowy Institute on sovereign borrowing costs.

For years, discussions of public debt used a fairly stable hierarchy. Advanced economies were treated as the safe core, while African sovereigns were grouped into a high-risk periphery. Jana de Kluiver and Henry Gilfillan point to a more complicated moment: long-term borrowing costs have risen sharply in some major Western economies, while yields for several African borrowers have declined.

This does not mean that the old hierarchy has reversed. It means that labels such as advanced and emerging are poor substitutes for current fiscal credibility, inflation dynamics, political stability, external balances, and the willingness of governments to make difficult adjustments.

The useful mental shift is from category to mechanism. Investors do not ultimately get repaid by a country’s place in a textbook taxonomy. They get repaid by institutions, revenues, reserves, monetary credibility, and political choices. Those variables can improve in countries with difficult histories and deteriorate in countries with strong reputations.

Together, these three economics pieces point toward the same discipline: do not confuse a high-level label with the path through which the outcome is produced. Trade war, AI investment, and emerging-market debt each hide a structure underneath.

Now to global politics and institutions.

Fourth, the Lowy Institute on BRICS and the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation.

Nick Bisley looks at the clustering of major BRICS and SCO meetings and argues that these institutions are gaining room as confidence in parts of the older international architecture weakens. That does not make them coherent blocs. Their members have different regimes, different economic interests, and in some cases direct strategic rivalries. But they still provide forums in which governments can coordinate, signal dissatisfaction with existing institutions, and expand their diplomatic options.

This distinction matters. Analysts often ask whether BRICS is becoming an anti-Western alliance. That is probably too demanding a test. Institutions can matter without producing NATO-like solidarity. A forum may be useful because it gives members bargaining leverage, new financial relationships, or another place to negotiate standards.

The more interesting question is therefore not whether these organizations replace the old order. It is whether they gradually reduce the monopoly of older institutions over agenda-setting. A world with more overlapping forums can become more plural without becoming neatly bipolar.

Fifth, Project Syndicate on preventing an AI world war.

S. Alex Yang and Angela Huyue Zhang focus on a specific security problem created by increasingly autonomous cyber systems. If an AI agent causes a serious intrusion or disruption without a clear human command, the victim may observe the damage without knowing whether it was deliberate. In a tense U.S.-China relationship, that ambiguity could become dangerous.

The authors argue that governance therefore needs more than general promises of responsible AI. States need mechanisms for attribution, communication, and distinguishing unintended machine behavior from intentional attack.

This is an old strategic problem in a new form. Nuclear rivals built hotlines and signaling practices because misperception could be catastrophic. Cyber systems have always complicated attribution, but more autonomous agents could make the link between state intent and operational effect even weaker.

The important point is that safety is not only about preventing a model from doing something harmful. It is also about preventing other actors from interpreting an ambiguous event in the most escalatory way.

Sixth, 天下雜誌 on why China has much less of an AI-doom narrative.

The article contrasts Chinese and American public attitudes toward AI. In China, AI is more commonly framed as an engine of development, industrial upgrading, and national progress. In the United States, discussion gives more space to catastrophic risk, loss of control, and the possibility that frontier systems should be slowed.

That difference is not just cultural color. It shapes the bargaining space for international governance.

If one side sees restraint as prudent risk management while the other hears it as an attempt to preserve an incumbent advantage, the same policy proposal carries different meanings. A safety mechanism that appears neutral in one political system may look strategic in another. The result is that technical cooperation has to solve a problem of interpretation as well as a problem of engineering.

Placed beside the Project Syndicate piece, the implication is useful: cooperation becomes easier when it starts from specific shared failure modes. How do we distinguish an accidental cyber event from a deliberate attack? is narrower than Should AI development slow down? Narrow questions can sometimes create confidence even when the larger philosophies remain far apart.

Now to science.

Seventh, Quanta Magazine on biology and quantum-like mathematics.

For decades, researchers have periodically suggested that quantum mechanics might explain puzzling features of living systems. Many such claims have been overextended. The piece by Elise Cutts describes a subtler possibility: biology may sometimes be modeled using mathematical structures associated with quantum theory even when the underlying biological system is not literally operating as a quantum device.

That is an important distinction. Mathematics is portable. A formalism developed in one field can describe patterns in another field without importing the original physical mechanism.

The attraction here is to systems in which the act or context of measurement changes what can be meaningfully said about the state of the system. Biological behavior can be highly context-dependent. Classical probability models sometimes assume a single underlying state from which all observations could, in principle, be derived. Quantum-style probability gives researchers another language for cases where the measurement context matters.

Whether this becomes broadly useful remains open. But the methodological lesson is strong: interdisciplinary borrowing works best when researchers are precise about what is being borrowed.

Eighth, Quanta on jumping genes.

A large fraction of the human genome is made of transposons, mobile genetic elements that can copy or move themselves. They were once easy to describe as parasites or genomic junk because they can disrupt genes and create mutations.

The modern picture is more interesting. Evolution repeatedly co-opts parts of these elements. Sequences that once served a selfish genetic function can become regulatory switches or contribute to new biological functions. A piece of DNA can therefore move from being a threat to being raw material.

That weakens one of the intuitions people often bring to biology: the idea that useful systems are assembled from components that were useful from the beginning. Evolution does not work like an engineer starting with clean parts. It inherits clutter, conflict, and historical accidents, then reuses whatever can be made to work.

This is one reason biological explanation often rewards historical thinking. The question is not only, What is this component for? It is also, What was it before, and how was it repurposed?

The two Quanta pieces fit together in an unexpected way. One is about formal reuse across scientific disciplines; the other is about functional reuse across evolutionary history. In both cases, something acquires a new role without carrying all of its original meaning with it.

Now to technology as lived infrastructure.

Ninth, Rest of World on China’s rise in health technology.

Viola Zhou interviews physician and biotech consultant Ruby Wang about a broad transformation in Chinese health technology. The interesting part is its scope. China’s advantage is not confined to one spectacular invention. It spans digital appointment systems, telemedicine, AI-assisted care, medical devices, biotech, drug development, manufacturing, and the ability to operate at very large scale.

That scale can be a learning system. Large patient populations and digitized care pathways generate experience. Manufacturing depth can reduce costs. Policy can push adoption. Weaknesses in traditional primary care can also create incentives to leap toward digital models rather than merely copying older systems.

For global readers, the important question is not whether China wins health tech. It is which Chinese approaches travel. Low-cost diagnostics, medical devices, digital workflows, and drugs can diffuse into countries that face different constraints from the United States or Europe.

Technology leadership can therefore emerge from solving operational problems at scale, not just from publishing frontier research.

Tenth, Rest of World on South African resistance to American data centers.

Danai Nesta Kupemba reports on civil-society groups challenging data-center expansion in South Africa. Their concerns are concrete: electricity, water, land, transparency, and the terms on which foreign technology companies gain access to scarce infrastructure.

This is a useful companion to the CEPR electricity-price research. One piece measures price effects in American utility territories. The other shows how similar infrastructure pressures become political in a country where resource constraints and inequality are already central public concerns.

The cloud metaphor encourages us to imagine computing as placeless. In reality, every large model rests on physical sites. Those sites compete with other users for power, water, transmission capacity, and sometimes public subsidies.

A global AI economy therefore creates local bargaining problems. Who gets priority when electricity is scarce? What compensation should communities receive? How transparent should utilities and developers be about future demand? Which infrastructure upgrades are public goods, and which are private project costs?

There is no universal answer, but ignoring those questions is itself a distributional choice.

Eleventh, The New Yorker on Liangzhu, China’s vibe-coding capital.

Afra Wang reports from a town near Hangzhou where former big-tech employees, young founders, and developers are using AI tools to build products with very small teams. The setting matters because it follows a difficult period for China’s technology labor market. Layoffs, weaker growth, and the end of the old high-salary expansion changed what entrepreneurship means.

AI lowers the cost of making software. A small group—or sometimes one person—can prototype products that previously required a larger team. That creates real autonomy. It also arrives at a moment when many workers have fewer attractive corporate options.

The result is a form of entrepreneurship that is both aspirational and defensive.

This complicates the familiar story that AI simply destroys jobs or simply creates new firms. It can do both through the same mechanism. Lower production costs expand what individuals can build, while also reducing the amount of labor required to build it.

The social meaning of an entrepreneurial boom therefore depends partly on what people are leaving behind. Starting a company after a lucrative corporate job is different from starting one because the corporate ladder has weakened.

Twelfth, Noema on letting citizens help set the pace of frontier AI.

Hélène Landemore and Audrey Tang start from a coordination problem. Even if major AI labs agree that development is moving too fast, no company wants to slow down first if competitors keep racing. The same logic applies to states worried about relative technological advantage.

Their proposed solution is deliberately ambitious: a standing global citizens’ assembly selected by lot, supported by independent expertise, able to receive evaluation results and require public responses from labs and governments.

The institutional proposal is contestable. Representativeness at planetary scale is difficult. Authority would have to be earned, and governments would still control the legal powers that make rules binding.

But the essay is valuable even if one rejects the proposed assembly. It asks who can legitimately speak for public interests when the main regulators are also strategic competitors and the main companies are also commercial participants.

That is a recurring governance problem well beyond AI.

Now, step back from the twelve pieces.

The first theme this week is hidden incidence: who actually pays.

Trade restrictions may be announced against one country, but the cost can land on intermediate producers in several others. Data centers may be financed by private firms, but some grid costs can land on households. Global AI demand may create valuable infrastructure, but communities may bear land, water, and electricity pressure.

Whenever a policy or technology is described as a single transaction, ask whether the incidence is actually distributed through a network.

The second theme is institutional optionality.

BRICS and the SCO do not need to become a disciplined alliance to change diplomacy. African sovereigns do not need to become safe in an absolute sense for markets to reassess them relative to richer borrowers. Chinese health technology does not need to replace American or European systems to widen the menu available to other countries.

Power often changes by adding options rather than by replacing one system with another.

The third theme is interpretation.

The United States and China can observe the same AI capability and attach different political meanings to it. A cyber event can look intentional even when an autonomous system produced it without a direct order. A mathematical formalism can look as if it carries the physical theory that originally produced it, even when it is being used only as an abstract tool.

In each case, misunderstanding comes from treating an observable form as proof of the mechanism underneath.

The fourth theme is reuse.

Evolution turns mobile genetic elements into useful regulation. Scientists reuse mathematical structures across fields. Workers reuse AI tools to rebuild careers after an older employment model weakens. Countries combine technologies from different geopolitical camps rather than choosing a single stack.

Real systems are opportunistic. They rarely respect the clean categories analysts create for them.

And the fifth theme is governance after the fact.

Technologies often diffuse faster than institutions adapt. Data centers arrive before cost-allocation rules are settled. Frontier models improve before international signaling mechanisms exist. Small AI-native companies proliferate before labor markets know how to value the work around them. Health technologies scale before other countries decide which components they want to import.

That does not mean governance always needs to stop deployment. It means institutions need to identify which externalities become expensive to correct later.

There is one more pattern worth drawing out: the difference between capacity and control.

A government can possess the capacity to impose a tariff without controlling how firms reorganize around it. A technology company can possess the capacity to build a data center without controlling how regulators allocate grid costs or how communities respond. A state can possess advanced AI systems without controlling how every autonomous action will be interpreted by a rival. A laboratory can possess a powerful model without controlling the legitimacy of the rules under which society wants that model deployed.

Capacity is often concentrated. Control is usually distributed.

That is a useful antidote to stories built around powerful actors. We naturally focus on presidents, chief executives, ministries, and frontier laboratories because their decisions are visible. But complex systems often behave according to the responses of actors who were not at the center of the original decision: suppliers, utility commissions, local governments, workers, consumers, investors, scientists, and rival institutions.

This helps explain why prediction becomes difficult precisely when a system looks most centralized. A powerful actor can change the incentives faced by everyone else, but the final outcome depends on how those others adapt.

For worldview purposes, that suggests a practical reading habit. When you encounter a strong causal claim, try separating it into four layers.

Layer one is the intervention. What actually changed? A tariff rate, a subsidy, a model capability, a new institution, a data center connection, a regulation.

Layer two is transmission. Through what channel is the intervention supposed to matter? Prices, expectations, supply constraints, reputational pressure, labor substitution, network effects, political legitimacy.

Layer three is adaptation. Who changes behavior because of the intervention, and what alternatives do they have? Firms can reroute supply chains. Households can reduce consumption. Governments can create new institutions. Workers can become founders. Biological systems can repurpose genetic elements over evolutionary time.

Layer four is equilibrium. After those adaptations, what system are we actually left with? This is usually where the result diverges from the slogan.

This four-layer habit is especially useful for technology stories because technological capability is only the first layer. The second through fourth layers determine social impact.

Take AI. Models become more capable is an intervention. Transmission might run through cheaper software production, automated cyber operations, faster medical analysis, or greater electricity demand. Adaptation then includes firms reorganizing teams, governments changing rules, utilities building infrastructure, workers learning new tools, and rivals changing strategy. The equilibrium could involve more productivity, more concentration, more entrepreneurship, more energy conflict, or some mixture of all four.

The same method applies to geopolitics. A new bloc holds a summit tells us almost nothing by itself. We need to know what members can do through that institution that they could not do before, whether those capabilities change outside actors’ behavior, and whether the arrangement persists when member interests diverge.

It also applies to markets. Yields fell is an observation, not an explanation. The meaningful question is which expectations changed: inflation, fiscal policy, political risk, external financing, or investor demand.

The pieces in this issue are valuable largely because they make those intermediate layers visible.

That is what a worldview publication should do. Not simply provide more events, but expose the machinery connecting events to outcomes.

A good rule of thumb is to ask three questions.

First: what becomes hard to reverse once adoption scales?

Second: which harms or benefits spill beyond the parties making the immediate decision?

Third: what evidence would tell us that the system is working differently from the story we tell about it?

Those questions connect nearly everything in this issue.

They also give us a useful counterweight to a common style of global analysis. It is easy to organize the world around contests: China versus America, technology versus regulation, markets versus states, incumbents versus challengers, growth versus safety.

The pieces this week repeatedly show why those binaries are incomplete.

China and the United States can compete while still needing shared mechanisms for accidental cyber events. States can support industrial resilience while firms continue to depend on transnational production networks. AI can empower one-person companies while weakening the labor demand that once supported large teams. Data centers can raise productivity while also shifting infrastructure costs onto people who do not own a share of the upside. A geopolitical forum can weaken Western institutional dominance without becoming a coherent enemy bloc.

The more useful unit of analysis is often the arrangement: the specific combination of incentives, institutions, resources, and feedback loops that produces behavior.

That is also why some of the strongest pieces in Worldview Weekly are not the most dramatic ones.

A four-minute analysis of sovereign yields can change how we think about country risk. A study of utility territories can reveal the political economy of AI infrastructure. A story about a small town outside Hangzhou can explain how macroeconomic slowdown and technological optimism coexist. A genetics explainer can make us more skeptical of clean categories such as parasite and partner.

The point is not to collect more facts. It is to improve the models we use to connect them.

So the question to carry into the next week is this:

When a major story is presented as a contest between two visible actors, what network of less visible actors, constraints, and institutions actually determines the outcome?
