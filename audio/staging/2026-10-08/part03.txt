Seventh, Quanta Magazine on sea monkeys and the rules of turbulence.

Brine shrimp, tiny creatures once sold as sea monkeys, provide an unexpectedly powerful way to study a deep question in fluid mechanics. When a fluid becomes turbulent, how does energy move from structures of one size to those of another?

Think about stirring a mug of water. In many three-dimensional flows, energy entering through a large motion passes through progressively smaller eddies until viscosity dissipates it as heat. In approximately two-dimensional turbulence, the pattern can differ: energy often moves toward larger structures instead. Physics textbooks describe these contrasting cascades as a consequence of the dynamics and conservation laws of different-dimensional flows.

A University of Pittsburgh team used tiny actively swimming shrimp to inject disturbances into a thin layer of fluid. The collective movements created a laboratory version of active turbulence. The scientists introduced a small obstacle, then changed its angle relative to the flow. With the right orientation they could alter, and even reverse, the direction of energy transfer. It was not enough to know that the experiment was approximately two-dimensional. The placement and shape of a boundary also mattered.

This does not overthrow the underlying mathematics of turbulence. It clarifies which assumptions are necessary before the familiar result follows. Active swimmers, local forcing and physical boundaries can make a textbook generalization inadequate as a description of a particular experiment. The laboratory is small precisely so researchers can change one feature at a time and see the consequences.

There are obvious limits to what has been shown. An aquarium-scale experiment does not establish that the same simple intervention will control an ocean current or an industrial processing line. Suggestions about pollution dispersion and mixing are research possibilities, not proven applications. But the experiment identifies a potential mechanism worth testing at other scales.

The broader intellectual lesson is about boundary conditions. Sometimes the most productive question is not whether a scientific rule is true in the abstract, but what physical arrangements make it true. When an observation reverses under a small change in geometry, a theoretical distinction that once seemed sufficient must be made more precise. That is how a surprising experiment can strengthen a field rather than simply undermine its established principles.

Eighth, another Quanta report, on electrical waves in the brain.

The brain coordinates vast numbers of neurons, but there is an unresolved disagreement over the status of certain collective patterns. Are traveling waves of electrical activity part of the machinery that organizes thought? Or do those waves mainly reveal the underlying neuron-to-neuron computation without independently driving it?

Researchers used to describe relatively simple bands of activity moving across the cortex. Newer recordings can reveal much more intricate forms. Some patients with severe epilepsy have electrodes implanted for medical diagnosis. With appropriate research participation, those recordings can reveal activity across regions of cortex while people carry out memory tasks. Other experiments use laboratory mice.

The observed patterns include spirals, waves radiating outward from a source, and patterns converging on a region. In one human study, rotating waves appeared relatively often during a challenging spatial-memory task; simpler patterns were more common during verbal-memory exercises. Mouse experiments have found coordinated rotation across the hemispheres and circuits whose geometry may support these patterns.

One hypothesis is that the waves provide a rapid layer of organization. Anatomical connections change relatively slowly, but a brain must move between tasks in seconds. Traveling electrical activity might change the excitability and timing of different areas, coordinating information flow without physically rewiring the network.

The competing account is important. Some neuroscientists argue that local electrical fields mostly reflect synaptic currents and coordinated firing. Under that model the neurons perform the computations while the wave patterns are useful signatures. Observing a spiral alongside spatial memory does not show that disrupting the spiral would disrupt memory. And electrodes in epilepsy patients sample locations chosen for medical reasons; they may not represent the whole cortex.

This is a distinction between association and causation. A thermometer tracks a weather system without causing it, while in other physical systems a field can feed back and influence its components. To decide which relationship applies, scientists need interventions, predictive theories and evidence that separates the hypotheses. A compelling visualization is a beginning, not an answer.

Quanta's account is valuable precisely because it does not turn a beautiful pattern into a promise to decode thoughts. It shows researchers disagreeing about a clear causal question, and it explains what additional evidence would actually advance that disagreement.
