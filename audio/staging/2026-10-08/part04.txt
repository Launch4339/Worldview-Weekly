Ninth, from Taiwan's CommonWealth Magazine, an essay on why Asian firms have pursued risky overseas projects, and why their next challenge may be different.

David Kim traces the history of South Korean engineering companies taking on work where established competitors hesitated. The familiar account emphasizes tenacity, ambition and a willingness to take chances. He argues that this misses the machinery that made persistence possible: export credit, political-risk insurance, government diplomacy, large corporate balance sheets and operating expertise. A project that would be intolerably risky for a standalone contractor could become financeable when those institutions divided the exposure.

Korean companies expanded across the Middle East, sometimes in war zones, while Taiwan pursued overseas petroleum investments despite having relatively few diplomatic allies. Some Taiwanese ventures remained active even after host governments switched diplomatic recognition to Beijing. China later scaled state-backed infrastructure investment through its Belt and Road program. These histories differ, but they share an understanding of long-duration commitment as a competitive capability.

Yet the author does not present a success story without costs. Contractors lost money, geopolitical changes rewrote partnerships and projects sometimes created uncomfortable relationships with governments. The distinctive argument comes in his discussion of a Himalayan hydropower disaster in Nepal in twenty twenty-six. The project reportedly had development-bank participation, insurance and engineering safeguards. Nevertheless, a destructive collapse involving mountain and glacier hazards exceeded important planning assumptions. An insurance design centered on earthquake measurements is not automatically a hedge against a glacier-related slope failure and flood. The article is an analytical opinion essay rather than a formal accident investigation, so the exact attribution of responsibility should be treated as provisional.

Political risk usually involves actors who can change behavior, accept concessions or respond to diplomacy. Physical hazards obey no negotiating table. Worse, their frequency may change as glaciers retreat and climatic conditions depart from the historical record used in engineering models. A project can have sophisticated financing and still carry a badly measured exposure.

The policy dilemma is more complicated than simply raising premiums. If lenders fully price uncertain future hazards and withdraw, poorer countries may lose needed power, transport and connectivity. Ignoring risks, however, leaves families and taxpayers paying later. Better hazard mapping, adaptable engineering, transparent contingency planning and public guarantees may have to work together.

This piece moves industrial policy away from a simple contest of national courage. It asks how financial institutions decide what a risk is, who bears it, and whether a system built to handle politics can learn to handle rapidly changing physical conditions. The lesson also connects to our South China Sea reading: reliable science needs a role before a crisis, not just after competing interests begin assigning blame.

Tenth, from Noema, Nathan Gardels revisits Germany's reunification and the political meaning of belonging.

Economic explanations for nationalism commonly emphasize wage losses, regional decline, unemployment and inequality. Those are substantial factors. Gardels asks what happens when people also lose familiar institutions, shared routines and a sense that they belong to a recognizable social world.

He revisits conversations from the early nineteen nineties, shortly after the collapse of communist East Germany. Thinkers including Heiner Müller and Daniel Cohn-Bendit warned that reunification's immense expansion of freedom would coexist with dislocation and disappointed expectations. Old jobs and institutions disappeared. Some eastern families expected prosperity and social status to converge with the west far faster than proved possible. Intellectuals feared that a vacuum of identity and trust would create opportunities for xenophobic movements.

Gardels sees those warnings as relevant to the present strength of radical nationalism in parts of eastern Germany. His argument is that a liberal society needs more than formal rights and economic efficiency. It also needs a civic sense of belonging, credible integration of newcomers and immigration rules the public can regard as legitimate.

There are serious objections to his diagnosis. Interviews from three decades ago cannot establish a causal model of contemporary voting. Economic conditions, political organization, propaganda and opportunistic leadership also matter. National attachment can be healthy, but the same language can be used to exclude citizens or newcomers on grounds that undermine equal rights. Acknowledging these distinctions is essential.

Nor does the diagnosis dictate one immigration policy. Some will argue that clearer border enforcement creates space for sustainable openness. Others will argue that education, housing, employment, integration services and stronger protections against discrimination are more directly effective in rebuilding trust. Both positions require evidence about actual outcomes, not only persuasive stories about national identity.

The article is worth reading as a historically informed interpretation, not as a settled finding. It adds a time dimension to present political disputes. An institutional transformation may be declared complete by leaders, investors and constitutional lawyers while the social experience of transformation remains unsettled for a generation. Policy can be more effective when it recognizes that people need both material opportunity and a meaningful place in a shared civic community.

Our final two readings connect those hidden costs back to technology.
