TITLE: Worldview Weekly — October 8, 2026

Worldview Weekly, for October eighth, twenty twenty-six.

This issue follows a deceptively simple question: when a government, company or institution promises to remove a risk, has it actually removed it, or merely moved the bill somewhere less visible?

Taiwan has sheltered households from energy-price increases, but its public energy companies and treasury now carry more of the burden. AI companies are buying computing power, while phone buyers in poorer countries encounter a more expensive memory-chip market. In the Pacific, foreign aid meant to secure alliances may reinforce domestic patronage. On the internet, thousands of miles of cables can create an illusion of redundancy if they share one dangerous stretch of sea. Even the evidence itself can be misleading: a decline relative to an employment trend is not the same as layoffs, and an electrical wave in the brain is not yet proof that the wave causes thought.

Our twelve readings come from seven publications. They range from macroeconomics and African security to Korean construction finance, fluid turbulence, and neuroscience. Most are from the past week; a couple were worth bringing forward because they sharpen the larger story. The links and short summaries are in the written Worldview Weekly feed. What follows is a deeper, original synthesis, not a substitute for the authors' reporting.

We begin with business and political economy.

First, from CEPR and VoxEU: Not all geopolitical shocks are inflationary, by economist Marco Pinchetti.

The word geopolitical often works as a mental shortcut in economic commentary. A war starts, tensions rise, insurance becomes more expensive, and the instinct is to expect higher inflation. That instinct was reinforced by the energy shock following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. But this week's article makes an essential distinction: geopolitical danger can reach the economy through both supply and demand, and the price consequences can point in opposite directions.

Picture two events. In the first, fighting threatens an oil-producing region or shipping lane. Traders anticipate lost supply. Oil prices rise even if no production has yet stopped, because the probability of a shortage is enough to change contracts and inventories. Firms that use energy pay more, and households face higher bills. Output suffers even while prices climb. Economists call this an adverse supply shock.

In the second event, a crisis frightens investors and businesses so badly that they cancel projects, postpone travel and purchases, and revise growth expectations downward. Oil prices may fall because expected demand for energy declines. Output again suffers, but this time inflationary pressure falls with it. That is closer to a demand or uncertainty shock.

Pinchetti tries to separate these mechanisms empirically instead of categorizing whole wars as if each contained one single shock. His starting point is a geopolitical risk index based on news coverage, paired with oil-market movements around major events since the nineteen eighties. He looks at the joint movement in a short, three-day window. When geopolitical risk rises alongside oil, the evidence points more toward an energy-supply channel. When risk rises and oil falls, it suggests a broader deterioration in anticipated demand. Those signals then help identify shocks in a structural macroeconomic model.

The estimates are modest in size but stark in direction. An energy-type shock depresses industrial production and raises consumer prices by around two-tenths of one percent at its peak. A demand-like geopolitical shock also weakens activity, but the estimated consumer-price response is negative, with declines of roughly one-tenth to four-tenths of a percent across horizons.

There are limits. Oil prices are not a perfect diagnostic instrument. Speculation, exchange rates, inventories and policy responses can affect them simultaneously. Three-day market reactions and a structural model do not give us an experimental partition of a complex event. And these are estimated responses to standardized shocks, not predictions for any particular new conflict. The contribution is conceptual and empirical: pooling all geopolitical episodes into an average conceals mechanisms that may cancel each other in the inflation data.

The practical implication is for central banks and governments. If prices are rising because energy supply has been impaired, raising interest rates will not create more oil, though it may prevent inflation from spreading into longer-term expectations. If the dominant channel is collapsing demand, aggressive tightening could intensify the contraction. The right diagnosis requires watching actual transmission channels, not simply measuring how alarming the news sounds. Keep this distinction in mind when we reach Taiwan's energy dilemma and the physical bottleneck in the Red Sea.

Second, also from CEPR and VoxEU: The adjustment of the Danish labour market to AI has begun.

The public debate over AI and jobs often pits two observations against each other. On one side, capabilities in writing, coding and analysis are improving quickly. On the other, national unemployment statistics do not show mass displacement attributable to AI. Both observations can be true if the early effect is not a wave of dismissals but a quiet reduction in hiring.

Three researchers, Simone Maria Bonin, Saman Darougheh and Andreas Kuchler, use Danish administrative records to look beneath the aggregate numbers. They link official firm surveys recording adoption of AI in twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four to monthly employer-employee data. That allows them to study employment paths at companies that report adopting the technology, and to compare those paths with firms that do not report adoption, accounting for earlier trends and industry timing.

Their headline result needs particularly careful wording. By September twenty twenty-five, companies that had adopted AI in twenty twenty-three had employment about nine percent below the path implied by their own earlier trend, relative to nonadopters. That does not mean nine percent of employees were laid off. Many of these companies were still growing. The point is that growth was slower than the earlier trajectory suggested.

Where did the difference arise? The researchers find much of it in recruitment. The stock of recently hired employees falls relative to trend while longer-tenured employees are retained. Young university graduates working in occupations with high AI exposure appear especially affected. Those occupations were about twenty-seven percent below their prior employment trend in the comparison, compared with roughly twelve percent for other roles. Among firms with fewer than one hundred workers, the overall gap was about sixteen percent. Larger adopting firms did not show a statistically significant decline. A separate cohort of firms first adopting in twenty twenty-four followed a similar delayed pattern, which makes the timing harder to dismiss as one peculiar event in twenty twenty-three.

What explains the small-firm concentration? One possibility is that AI lets a small team accomplish more without proportionally expanding its staff. But the authors also note that capital constraints may matter: smaller firms that spend scarce resources on implementing new technology may have less left for payroll, at least initially. Their ongoing work finds a concentration among already leveraged businesses. A company can buy software, reorganize processes, and delay hiring without the software itself having causally replaced an employee. The study is observational. Early adopters may differ from nonadopters in ambition, business prospects, financing conditions or management quality. Its estimates should not be recited as a controlled causal effect of AI.

Still, the implication is consequential. Labor-market adjustment can be intergenerational. The person who has a job today may keep it, while the graduate who would have entered that occupation next year finds fewer openings. Over time, fewer junior positions can weaken the pipeline through which workers acquire judgment, tacit knowledge and promotion opportunities. That creates a different set of policy questions from the standard debate about retraining the unemployed. We may need to examine entry-level hiring, apprenticeships and professional development even when layoffs remain subdued.

The Danish evidence is early and covers a limited slice of the economy. It may or may not generalize to labor markets with different protections and business models. But it is a better starting question than asking only whether an AI unemployment crisis has appeared. Look at the hiring margin, not only the stock of jobs.

Third, from Taiwan's CommonWealth Magazine: Who Pays to Keep Taiwan's Energy Prices Down?

Think of Taiwan as an economy trying to satisfy three objectives at once: cushion households against global fuel-price shocks, remain competitive in energy-intensive semiconductor production, and maintain reliable imported-energy supplies. Each objective is defensible. It is their combination that becomes expensive.

CommonWealth traces the bill through the accounts of CPC, Taiwan's state-owned fuel company, and the government's new budget proposal. On October first, the administration proposed close to four hundred fifteen billion New Taiwan dollars in energy-related support. Roughly one hundred eighty-one billion would compensate energy suppliers for costs they absorbed under price-stabilization policies. Another roughly two hundred thirty-four billion would go into CPC as fresh capital to stabilize its finances and support infrastructure. Those are not the same financial operation. A subsidy covers an expense; a capital injection rebuilds a balance sheet. Both ultimately rely on public resources.

CPC has recorded losses for six consecutive years. The government estimates accumulated losses could approach one hundred twenty-eight billion New Taiwan dollars by the end of twenty twenty-six, close to CPC's existing paid-in capital. The company has disputed headlines predicting its bankruptcy next year. That distinction matters: a state-backed enterprise with an explicit rescue plan is not the same as a private business about to shut its doors. What is established is the deterioration and the need for government support, not a guaranteed corporate collapse.

Why the losses? Taiwan has repeatedly prevented higher world oil and natural-gas costs from appearing immediately in domestic prices. The article counts twenty-one price freezes for gasoline and diesel this year, additional limits on increases, and frozen household gas charges. This protects purchasing power and restrains measured inflation in the short run. It does not repeal the international price. The uncollected revenue or uncompensated cost sits first with the supplier, then potentially with taxpayers, future consumers or other public spending priorities.

Taiwan's semiconductor success complicates the arithmetic. From twenty nineteen to twenty twenty-five, electricity consumption in electronics manufacturing rose by thirty-five percent. Demand from factories and advanced packaging is rising even as Taiwan remains heavily dependent on imported fuel. In recent years, weaker electricity demand from nontechnology industries offset part of the increase. That is not a permanent energy strategy.

There is no effortless policy alternative. Letting prices jump overnight can hurt lower-income households, trigger politically destabilizing inflation and burden productive companies. Subsidies can sensibly distribute a temporary shock over time. But a temporary buffer becomes a structural problem when the state company is expected to absorb repeated crises indefinitely. Targeted support, investment in supply resilience, gradual price signals and transparent fiscal accounting can be combined in different ways. Those choices have distributional consequences; none makes the resource cost vanish.

This story also changes how to think about industrial policy. Taiwan's manufacturing advantage is not only about excellent engineers, chipmaking equipment or geopolitical alliances. It rests on grid capacity, imported natural gas, regulated prices and the creditworthiness of a public enterprise. Anyone analyzing the future of advanced chips while ignoring those foundations is analyzing only the visible tip of the system.
