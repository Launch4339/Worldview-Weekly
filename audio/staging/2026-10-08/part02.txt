Global politics and institutions.

Fourth, from The New Yorker, Ishaan Tharoor on why the fighting in Ethiopia belongs to a larger regional conflict.

It is easy for coverage of an African civil war to stop at a country's border. There is a capital, a government, a rebel force, a ceasefire and perhaps an international mediator. In Ethiopia, that tidy structure now obscures as much as it explains.

The new fighting follows the collapse of a peace that had ended the devastating Tigray war in twenty twenty-two. The prior conflict took an enormous civilian toll, though estimates of deaths remain uncertain and difficult to verify. Tensions among Ethiopia's central government, the Tigray People's Liberation Front and other armed actors never truly disappeared. But what makes the current violence especially dangerous is that the alignment of neighboring countries and outside backers has changed.

Eritrea illustrates the problem. Its soldiers once fought alongside Ethiopia's central government against Tigrayan forces. It now appears to be on the opposite side of that contest. Ethiopia and Eritrea have broken diplomatic relations, while Ethiopia's leadership has accused Egypt and Sudanese actors of fomenting rebellion. The accused governments deny those charges. Sudan's own civil war involves two major armed camps, the regular army and the Rapid Support Forces. Reporting about assistance and cross-border collaboration is contested in places, so we should distinguish documented diplomatic positions from allegations about covert sponsorship.

Even if we strip out disputed intelligence, three structural sources of tension remain. First, Ethiopia is landlocked and wants more reliable access to the Red Sea. Its attempts to establish ties with Somaliland alarm Somalia, which considers Somaliland part of its sovereign territory. Second, Egypt and Ethiopia are divided over water and the implications of Ethiopia's large dam on the Nile. Third, the Red Sea and the Horn attract outside security interests from Gulf states, Turkey and other powers, with ports, bases, trade routes and resources at stake.

The result is not one coordinated conspiracy. It is a set of overlapping security dilemmas. A decision taken in Addis Ababa can alarm Cairo. A port proposal can reshape calculations in Mogadishu. An alliance in the Sudanese war can affect a neighboring insurgency. When the parties are fighting on different maps, a ceasefire drawn on only one of those maps may not last.

This is where the article's international-order argument has force. Institutions are supposed to offer negotiation, humanitarian access and enforceable settlements. But if regional patrons calculate that they gain more from sustaining an ally than from imposing restraint, institutions struggle to change incentives. External military support need not be omnipotent to be destabilizing. It only has to reduce the cost to a participant of continuing the fight.

An alternative reading should also be heard. These conflicts have deep local histories; reducing them to a proxy war of foreign powers would deny the agency of Ethiopians, Sudanese, Eritreans and Somalis, including civilians working for peace. Nor is every new port agreement evidence of war planning. What matters is recognizing that internal and external causes operate together. A durable settlement would have to make several governments see a reason to restrain their allies, respect each other's core interests and allow people to rebuild. That is harder than brokering a photo opportunity between two negotiating teams.

Fifth, from the Lowy Institute, Mohd Yunus on dying coral reefs in the South China Sea.

Most discussion of the South China Sea is organized around control: which country occupies which reef, whether a ship has crossed a line, what an arbitral ruling says, and how maritime power is distributed. The Lowy essay adds a reality that does not care about those lines. Coral reefs, fish stocks, larvae, sediment and water quality interact across the contested maritime space.

China's ecological assessment in August reported that coral cover around Sandy Cay had fallen by almost seventy percent compared with twenty sixteen. China and the Philippines have each accused the other of damaging the environment. Large-scale Chinese reclamation earlier in the decade transformed submerged reefs into artificial islands with military facilities; Vietnam and the Philippines have also carried out reclamation in areas they control. Sediment from dredging can damage habitats beyond the immediate project. A twenty sixteen arbitration held that China had breached obligations to protect marine ecosystems, while China rejected the award and its jurisdiction.

The essay's central observation is institutional. Scientific reports are increasingly written or deployed as evidence of national innocence or an opponent's guilt. That does not mean the scientific data are worthless. It means incentives around their collection and use are skewed toward litigation and political messaging rather than a shared account of environmental decline. Governments argue about who owns a reef while the reef continues to deteriorate.

Yunus proposes an intentionally modest alternative. Strengthen joint ecological surveys between ASEAN and China, involve technically credible independent institutions, standardize sampling and reporting, and maintain shared data on coral, water and fisheries. Most importantly, participants would join under a without-prejudice clause: collecting samples together, exchanging data or entering a site would neither acknowledge nor surrender any sovereign claim.

This is a useful idea because it tries to separate an urgent technical problem from a dispute too entrenched for easy settlement. Fisheries and ecosystems provide a form of common interest without requiring a fictional shared political identity. But the hard part is enforcement. Who chooses survey sites? Who verifies that militaries and dredging operations obey environmental restrictions? Can independent scientists gain safe access to all relevant locations? Will states publish unfavorable results? A technical body needs lasting funding, audit rights and enough autonomy to withstand the next diplomatic confrontation.

The larger lesson is about making progress under disagreement. Cooperation can be designed not to settle the underlying conflict, but to prevent a third party—in this case an ecosystem—from paying the full cost while people argue. A country need not change its sovereignty position to agree on monitoring coral mortality. Whether powerful claimants will accept a credible common record is the political test.

Sixth, also from Lowy's Interpreter, Mary-Louise O'Callaghan on Australia's proposed funding for Solomon Islands legislators.

The strategic backdrop is familiar. Australia worries about China's growing security influence in the Pacific. Solomon Islands leaders need infrastructure, jobs and services. Canberra wants long-term security cooperation, and development finance can help bring partners together. On paper, diplomacy and assistance may reinforce each other.

O'Callaghan examines a reported arrangement that reveals how easily they can instead work at cross-purposes. A diplomatic message verified and published by Australian media reportedly described a plan to make about ninety-three million Australian dollars available through the constituencies of Solomon Islands' fifty members of Parliament. The plan would be part of a much larger package connected with security treaty negotiations. Neither government had officially confirmed all the leaked details when the analysis was published. This remains a reported proposal, not an enacted program whose effects can be measured.

Why is funding through lawmakers different from building a road through an independently audited procurement system? Because each member could become the gatekeeper for benefits in their electorate. Constituency projects may certainly meet real needs, and locally chosen investments can be appropriate. But in a political system where patronage already shapes power, discretionary resources can reward personal loyalty rather than broad public benefit. Aid can become a mechanism for keeping coalition members in office, even if the donor's stated goal is institutional development.

There is also a democratic legitimacy problem. If a foreign country offers resources to politically influential actors while bargaining over security commitments, opponents may plausibly characterize the money as buying a strategic alignment. That accusation can weaken the very alliance it is meant to secure. The short-term reward of securing an agreement may come at the long-term cost of mistrust and weaker public institutions.

A realist might respond that the alternative is worse. If Australia refuses to offer useful resources, rival powers may step in, and Solomon Islands citizens could receive less investment and less security cooperation. That argument cannot be dismissed with a generic appeal to good governance. But it does not establish that every delivery channel is equally effective. The relevant comparison is not aid versus no aid; it is whether aid could be structured to provide visible constituency benefits under transparent eligibility rules, public accounting and independent review, rather than reinforcing individual patrons.

The case illustrates a recurrent trap in foreign policy. The donor treats the partner country as a location on a strategic map; citizens experience the bargain through their political institutions. A program that looks like leverage from Canberra may look like favoritism in Honiara. If a security agreement is to last across elections and leadership changes, its legitimacy inside the smaller country is not an optional afterthought. It is part of its strategic value.
